The Noise Plane: A Diffusion Bridge Across
Exponent and Dispersion
Xuran Hu, Yuru Jia, Tengxi Wang, Miloš Dakovi´c, Member, IEEE, Ljubiša Stankovi´c, Fellow, IEEE,
and Wufan Zhao, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Diffusion-based denoising commonly uses Gaussian
perturbations, whereas sensor noise follows diverse signal-
dependent statistical laws. In this paper, we propose p-Bridge, a
unified measurement-indexed diffusion framework for Tweedie
noise. The noise exponent p describes the variance–intensity
relation, while accumulated measurement ν controls the noise level.
Together, they define a noise plane that includes Gaussian, Poisson,
and Gamma models. The stochastic bridge connects measurement
distributions through independent increments, giving denoising a
physical interpretation as progressive measurement accumulation.
Both the starting and stopping indices can be specified, allowing
restoration from an observed measurement level to a prescribed
higher level. Measurement superposition yields a shared reverse-
mean update, paired with a deviance-based objective for learning
the required conditional mean. A latent compound Poisson–
Gamma construction enables sampling at intermediate exponents.
A single exponent-conditioned network adapts to different Tweedie
noise models without separate training for each exponent.
Experiments show that exponent conditioning improves cross-
exponent robustness over fixed-exponent models. Using estimated
exponents, the shared network achieves robustness comparable
to an oracle-selected set of five specialist models. Experiments on
synthetic images, FMD fluorescence microscopy, and multi-sensor
SAR images further demonstrate its effectiveness across different
signal-dependent noise regimes.
Index Terms—diffusion bridge, image restoration, denoising,
exponential dispersion models, Tweedie family.

MAGE denoising is a fundamental problem in computational
imaging, where the objective is to recover scene information
from measurements corrupted during acquisition. Classical
methods combine an observation model with image priors
based on sparsity or nonlocal similarity [1]. These approaches
established effective ways to separate image structure from
noise, while subsequent plug-and-play methods incorporated
learned denoisers into model-based optimization [2]. Their
formulation requires both a useful prior for the clean image
and an appropriate statistical description of the observation.
In physical imaging, however, the observation statistics vary
substantially across sensing mechanisms. Photon counting pro-
duces signal-dependent fluctuations in cameras and fluorescence

Learning-based methods have substantially improved denois-
ing by learning image statistics and restoration mappings from
data. Convolutional networks provide efficient inference [6],
[7], Transformer-based models capture long-range image
dependencies [8], and noise-conditioned models allow one
network to handle different corruption levels [9]. Nevertheless,
supervised restoration depends on how well the training data
represent the deployment conditions, while collecting accurate
clean references can be difficult in real photography and
scientific imaging [10], [11]. Learning from noisy observations
reduces this dependence. Noise2Noise uses corrupted training
targets [12], and Noise2Self exploits noise independence to
learn without clean targets [13]. These methods broaden the
available training settings, but their validity and performance
still depend on the statistical structure of the observations.
Blind restoration also benefits from explicitly estimating the
degradation. VIRNet, for example, jointly estimates noise
and restores the image under a spatially varying Gaussian
likelihood [14]. Such developments make denoising more
adaptive, while leaving the choice of the underlying noise
distribution an important modeling decision.
A complementary line of research addresses this modeling
decision through sensor physics and noise transformations.
Physics-based noise models describe the acquisition process
and generate realistic training pairs [15], while recent physics-
informed approaches learn difficult-to-model sensor compo-

methods improve the connection between synthetic training

offer another route by reducing signal dependence before
Gaussian denoising [17]–[19]. Noise2VST extends this idea
by learning a model-free transformation from the noisy input
and using a frozen Gaussian denoiser [20]. This demonstrates
that a Gaussian denoiser can remain useful for non-Gaussian
observations when coupled with suitable adaptation. These
approaches focus on modeling or transforming an observation
without specifying how its distribution should evolve along a
denoising trajectory.
Diffusion-based restoration makes this trajectory an explicit
part of the model. Restoration bridges such as I2SB learn pro-
cesses connecting degraded and clean image distributions [21],
while diffusion posterior sampling incorporates measurement
likelihoods, including Poisson noise, into reconstruction with
a diffusion prior [22]. Beyond Gaussian formulations, Xie
et al. construct denoising diffusion processes for Gaussian,
Gamma, and Poisson noise with a unified training strategy [23].
Gamma Bridge further gives the trajectory a physical coordinate
through the number of looks, allowing restoration between
specified measurement levels for Gamma speckle [24]. These
advances establish both noise-aware diffusion and measurement-
indexed restoration, but these constructions treat particular
noise laws or discrete families. Extending this perspective to
a continuously varying variance–intensity relation requires a
common construction that adapts the noise law while preserving
the measurement interpretation of the intermediate states.
In this work, we propose p-Bridge, a unified measurement-
indexed diffusion bridge based on the Tweedie family [25],
[26]. Our starting point is that accumulating independent
measurements of the same scene reduces noise while preserving
the expected intensity. The Tweedie variance relation µp/ν
allows us to apply this principle across different noise laws.
Here, p describes how variance depends on intensity, while ν
represents accumulated exposure, dose, or number of looks.
These two coordinates distinguish changes in the noise law
(Figure 1) from movement along a measurement trajectory
(Figure 2). At a fixed exponent, increments that are independent
conditional on the clean image connect the measurement
distributions into a stochastic bridge.
In this construction, the network predicts the clean signal
needed for each reverse update, while exponent conditioning
allows its prediction to adapt to the observation’s noise statistics.
We evaluate the role of this adaptation through controlled
exponent-mismatch and estimation experiments, and assess
restoration quality on synthetic images, FMD fluorescence
microscopy, and SAR images from multiple sensors.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Diffusion models, bridges and inverse problems
Widely used denoising diffusion models [27] and their score-
based formulations [28] add Gaussian noise according to a
prescribed variance schedule. Subsequent work has improved
sampling [29] and network parameterization [30]. Restoration-
oriented methods also redesign the trajectory. For example,
ResShift introduces residual shifting to reduce the number of
sampling steps [31].
Restoration bridges connect clean and degraded image
distributions. Schrödinger bridge formulations connect pre-
scribed endpoint distributions [32], with I2SB applying this
principle to image restoration [21]. Other formulations use
direct endpoint interpolation [33] or mean-reverting stochastic
differential equations [34]. More recent developments target
spatial adaptation and efficiency. RDBM uses endpoint resid-
uals to modulate noise injection and removal across image
regions [35], while E-Bridge shortens the trajectory and learns

System-Embedded Diffusion Bridge Models incorporate the
forward system explicitly, embedding a known linear operator
and noise covariance into a matrix-valued SDE under an
additive Gaussian measurement model [37].
Another class of methods adapts a pretrained diffusion
prior to the observation. DDRM uses the singular-value
decomposition of the forward operator [38], whereas DPS
introduces an approximate likelihood score [22]. DPS supports
Poisson measurement noise while retaining a Gaussian dif-
fusion prior. IRBridge transfers pretrained generative priors
to restoration bridges by relating processes with a common
endpoint distribution [39].
Non-Gaussian constructions extend the choice of diffusion
distributions. Xie et al. formulate diffusion denoising for
Gaussian, Gamma, and Poisson corruption with a unified
training strategy [23]. Learning to Jump uses count thinning and
reverse count thickening for generative modeling [40], while
Star-Shaped DDPM develops non-Gaussian models within
exponential families [41]. In statistical inference, data thinning
splits observations from convolution-closed distributions, in-
cluding Gaussian, Poisson, and Gamma laws, into independent
components [42]. Gamma Bridge indexes a speckle-denoising
trajectory by the number of looks [24]. p-Bridge extends this
measurement-indexed formulation to the Gaussian point and the
Poisson-to-Gamma continuum, using a common superposition
construction and a single exponent-conditioned network.
B. Signal-dependent denoising and noise estimation
Variance-stabilizing transformations reduce signal depen-
dence before Gaussian denoising. Representative choices
include the Anscombe transform for Poisson data [17], with
a corrected inverse at low counts [43], and the logarithmic
transform for speckle [19]. Noise2VST learns a model-free
transform from a single noisy image and combines it with
frozen Gaussian denoisers [20].
Sensor-specific methods incorporate the statistics of the
acquisition process. Photon-limited methods use variance-
stabilized nonlocal regression for microscopy [4], raw-data
networks [44], or physics-based sensor noise models [45].
Speckle methods exploit its multiplicative statistics [5] through
self-supervised networks [46] or closed-form shrinkage [47].
Single-image noise estimation is often formulated within a
specified noise family. Gaussian noise estimators use patch
statistics and covariance eigenvalues [48]. Poisson–Gaussian
fitting uses local mean–variance relations to estimate shot-
noise and read-noise parameters from a raw frame [3]. VIRNet
jointly estimates degradation and restores the image under
a spatially varying Gaussian likelihood [14]. Blind2Sound
couples noise-level sensing with self-supervised denoising for
Poisson–Gaussian corruption [49]. In p-Bridge, the estimated
exponent p selects the bridge’s measurement law, while the
estimated index ν determines its starting noise level. The two
parameters are fitted jointly as the scaling of ν depends on p.
III. A UNIFIED MEASUREMENT-INDEXED BRIDGE
In p-Bridge, denoising is modeled as the accumulation of
independent measurements of the same scene, which reduces
noise while preserving the underlying signal. We use the
accumulated measurement amount to index a bridge across
Gaussian, Poisson, Gamma, and intermediate noise models.
The exponent p selects the noise model, while the measurement
index ν controls its noise level. We first construct this bridge,
then derive its reverse update and reconstruction loss, and
finally describe sampling and training. Figure 3 shows the
network pipelines.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate p-Bridge on synthetic noise spanning the
exponent and severity axes, followed by real fluorescence
microscopy and SAR data. The experiments examine exponent
mismatch and conditioning, compare restoration methods, and
evaluate noise parameter estimation and robustness to exponent.
A. Experimental setup
Training data. We use a pool of 1,100 clean natural images
from BSDS500 and DIV2K [53], [54], with 256×256 training
crops. Clean intensities are mapped to X = [0.05, 1] using
the intensity offset. Noisy observations are generated from the
corresponding Tweedie model.
Synthetic evaluation. We evaluate on 64 held-out crops with
noise exponents pdata ∈{1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2} and severities
η ∈{0.25, 0.5, 1} (mild, moderate, and severe). Each fixed-
exponent model is tested across all five exponents, with
results averaged over three corruption seeds. Comparisons
with external methods use pdata ∈{1, 1.5, 2}.
Real-data evaluation. Noise calibration covers 12 FMD

On the 5 × 5 evaluation grid, the matched model achieves

D. Ablation studies
Evaluation protocol. Table VI summarizes the component
ablations of the conditional model. Each variant changes one
setting while keeping the others fixed. Training-side variants
are retrained with the same data and budget, whereas inference-
only variants reuse the full model’s checkpoint.
Loss weighting and index schedule. Measurement-dependent
weighting and a linear index schedule reduce PSNR by
0.345 and 0.268 dB, respectively. Although the mean re-
construction weight is unchanged, measurement-dependent
weighting assigns greater weight to cleaner states. Likewise,
a linear schedule allocates fewer training steps near the noisy
observation than a geometric schedule over the same index
range. These results support the use of constant weighting
and geometric spacing to emphasize noisy states under a fixed
training budget.
Regularization and reverse update. At five network evalua-
tions, removing consistency regularization and replacing the
reverse mixture with direct prediction reduce PSNR by 0.210
and 0.186 dB, respectively. These losses increase to 0.452
and 1.654 dB at 25 evaluations. At one evaluation, no reverse

Noise parameter estimation. We estimate the noise parameters
directly from the noisy image without additional training. Tak-
ing logarithms of Var(xobs | µ) = µp/ν gives a linear relation
with slope p and intercept −log ν. We fit a robust regression of
estimated log-noise variance on log-mean intensity. The fitted
slope and intercept jointly determine p and ν, with dispersion
absorbed into the measurement index. Details of local-statistic
estimation and regression are provided in Section S10-D.
On the evaluation grid of five exponents, three noise levels,
and three corruption seeds, the reported worst-case absolute
error in p is 0.0914, below the 0.25 target identified above.
The median relative error in ν ranges from 2.8% to 4.1%.
Mild noise is more challenging to estimate because, at a fixed
exponent, ν is 16 times its severe-noise value, leaving a weaker
noise signal. Additional tests of estimation bias and sensitivity
to estimator settings are provided in Section S9-B.

V. CONCLUSION
p-Bridge connects statistical noise modeling with the design
of a denoising trajectory. Measurement accumulation provides
the common bridge structure, while exponent conditioning
adapts the learned prediction to the noise law. The experiments
show that this adaptation matters beyond selecting a noise
level. Fixed-exponent models lose accuracy under mismatched
noise laws, whereas a shared conditional network achieves


13
cross-exponent robustness comparable to oracle-selected spe-
cialists. Using estimated exponents leaves this robustness
nearly unchanged, and moderate conditioning errors preserve
most of the benefit. Results on synthetic images, fluorescence
microscopy, and SAR further support the usefulness of the
formulation across imaging regimes. Together, these findings
support adapting to different Tweedie noise models by making
the noise law an explicit condition within a shared restoration
model, without a separate checkpoint for each exponent.

S8. EVALUATION CONVENTIONS
We provide additional results on the sampling budget and
worst-case evaluation.
A. Number of function evaluations
Table S2 evaluates fixed-exponent models with one, five,
and 25 network evaluations at severe noise. For matched
exponents, five evaluations achieve a mean PSNR of 22.330 dB,
compared with 22.326 dB for one evaluation and 22.225 dB for
25. Dark-region log-RMSE is also lowest at five evaluations
(0.2831), compared with 0.2841 and 0.2854 at one and 25
evaluations, respectively. Additional evaluations are more
helpful under exponent mismatch. From one to 25 evaluations,
the minimum PSNR of the fixed p = 1.5 model across
tested data exponents increases by 0.855 dB, while the mean
mismatch penalty decreases from 1.601 to 0.924 dB. Thus,
longer sampling reduces mismatch losses but does not improve
matched-exponent PSNR in this comparison.
We further evaluate the conditional model with NFE ∈
{1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 15, 25} on a held-out split. PSNR peaks at three
evaluations and decreases slightly thereafter. Relative to this
peak, one, five, and 25 evaluations reduce PSNR by 0.021,
0.006, and 0.092 dB, respectively. All runs use deterministic
sampling, and varying the projection onto X changes PSNR by


8
at most 0.002 dB. We retain five evaluations as the common
sampling budget, with a gap of only 0.006 dB to the best
setting in this sweep.
B. Evaluation across noise exponents
Mean mismatch loss and worst-case PSNR describe different
aspects of exponent robustness. The former summarizes the cost
of mismatched model–data exponent pairs and depends on the
evaluation grid. At severe noise with five network evaluations,
it is 2.000 dB on the 3 × 3 grid and 1.256 dB on the 5 × 5
grid. The latter is the minimum PSNR across the tested data
exponents. We use this common criterion in Table II to compare
fixed-exponent models with the conditional network. Among
the fixed models, the minimax model maximizes this minimum.

C. Noise-severity transfer
For noise-severity transfer, only the conditioning input is
thinned to the training index νobs, while the observed image
used to initialize sampling is unchanged. For 1 < p < 2,
synthetic evaluations use simulator-retained latent counts for
this thinning step.
D. Noise-exponent estimation
Algorithm S1 summarizes the single-image estimator. The
cross-difference residual removes locally affine signals. Under
independent pixel noise, its variance equals the average noise
variance of the four pixels. A 15 × 15 box filter supplies the
local mean intensities used for binning. Retaining the flattest
30% within each of 10 bins limits the contribution of image
structure to the variance estimates, which are then used in the
Theil–Sen fit. The pseudocode returns the regression estimates
before the range clipping described in the main paper.
E. Baseline configurations
I2SB and ResShift use our UNet and training pool. ResShift
operates in pixel space with five stochastic sampling steps.
DDRM uses an unconditional DDPM prior trained on the
clean pool. On synthetic data, its scalar noise level is computed
from the true per-pixel variance. Noise2VST fits a transform
to each test image for 2,000 iterations with frozen pretrained
denoisers. Its reported NFE = 1 counts the final denoising
pass. EAMamba uses 128×128 training crops, while evaluation
uses 256 × 256 crops.
