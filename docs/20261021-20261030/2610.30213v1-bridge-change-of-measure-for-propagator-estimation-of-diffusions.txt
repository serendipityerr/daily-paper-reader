Abstract The propagator of one-dimensional diffusions has relevant applications in sta-
tistical inference and across many disciplines. Yet it is obtained in closed form only for
very few simple models. Here we analyze an importance sampling strategy based on a
bridge change of measure to estimate propagators: paths are generated under an auxiliary
diffusion whose bridge between the two fixed boundary points is easy to generate, and the
target propagator is recovered through a Radon–Nikodym derivative or likelihood ratio
correction derived from Girsanov’s theorem. This method overcomes the main limitations
of the crude Monte Carlo estimator, which relies on binning in state space, introducing
bias and becoming inefficient with rare transitions. We apply the method to a range of
benchmark processes and show that the bridge-based estimator matches the accuracy of
crude Monte Carlo with orders of magnitude fewer sample paths. Finally, we elaborate on
discretization schemes for the Radon–Nikodym derivative and on approximations to the
best-in-class change of measure, both of which increase the efficiency of the method.

ratio – Markov property – Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process – Radon-Nikodym derivative – Rare
transition – Wiener process

I. INTRODUCTION
The propagator represents the major tool for the analysis of the distributional properties
of Markov processes. Since it encapsulates all the probabilistic information about the evo-
lution of the process, it is a central object in the physical, biological, economic and social
sciences where such models are used Azaele et al. (2016), C. W. Gardiner (1985), Risken
(1996), van Kampen (2007). Moreover, the propagator is the important component for the
construction of the likelihood of Markov models, making it essential for both Bayesian and
maximum likelihood inference.

differential equation (SDE) of the form

The general issue with the definition of the propagator in Eq. (3) and its PDE in Eq. (4) is
that they lead to explicit solution in a few limited cases only. The inaccessibility of the closed-
form expression of the propagator - and, consequently, of the likelihood - is widely regarded as
one of the major problems of statistical inference with SDEs; cf. e.g. Craigmile et al. (2023),
Iacus (2008), Sørensen (2004). Classical approaches to circumvent this difficulty include
approximating the propagator, by numerically integrating Eq. (4) Toral and Colet (2014),
through expansions in orthogonal functions Ait-Sahalia (2002) or by employing Monte Carlo
(MC) methods.
The aim of this work is to provide a rigorous analysis of a Monte Carlo method recently
used in Aguilar et al. (2026) for statistical inference on model parameters. Specifically, we
prove the main results on which the method rely and extend its range of applicability. This
method belongs to the family of importance sampling or change-of-measure techniques and
uses change-of-measure methods combined with bridge processes and their propagators.
The structure of the article is the following. In Sec. II, the Crude Monte Carlo propaga-
tor estimator is discussed. In Sec. III, the Bridge change-of-measure Monte Carlo estimator
(BCMMCE) of the propagator is introduced. Two discretization schemes for the evaluation
of a path integral appearing in the BCMMCE are presented in Sec. IV. On the practical side,
Sec. V provides the two algorithms of Bridge change-of-measure Monte Carlo propagator
estimation: the estimation algorithm with Wiener bridges in Sec. V.A and the estimation
algorithm with arbitrary Markov bridges in Sec. V.B. Then, Sec. VI suggests a parametric

approximation to the best-in-class bridge measure. Sec. VII presents the results of the nu-
merical study. Applications of Wiener bridges to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, Cox-Ingersol-Ross
and stochastic logistic target processes are presented in Sec. VII.A. Applications of Ornstein-
Uhlenbeck bridges to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and stochastic logistic target processes are
given in Sec. VII.B. The approximation to a parametric best-in-class Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
bridge is given in Sec. VII.C and comparisons of path integral discretizations can be found
in Sec. VII.D. Sec. VIII concludes the article.

Theorem 2 yields the algorithms presented in this section. As noted after Theorem 2,
the BCMMC technique is particularly well suited to sampling processes Y whose bridges
are easy to simulate and whose propagators are known. In practice, however, requiring Y to
share the volatility function B of the target process X is often too restrictive. We therefore
14

transform.

bility of the BCMMC technique, we also derived an approximate optimal choice of bridge
parameters within a chosen parametric class, optimality being in the sense of variance re-
duction. We developed a new specific discretization scheme for the path integrals appearing
in the BCMMCE. These improvements reduce both the statistical and bias errors, thereby
increasing the computational efficiency of the method. Finally, we presented several numeri-
cal illustrations that demonstrate the usefulness of the approach. A Python implementation
of the method, powered with NJIT for fast computations is shared at Ref. Aguilar (2026).
Such code is easy to adapt to solve arbitrary one-dimension diffusions.
Further contributions are the following. First, we identified two key advantages of the
BCMMC over the CMC for propagator estimation. First, BCMMC removes the Dirac delta
from the expectation defining the propagator, upon which CMC is based, thereby avoid-
ing the need for the state space discretization of CMC. Second, Corollary 1 shows that a
measure that minimizes the variance of the importance sampling propagator estimator is
a bridge measure, suggesting that bridge processes are particularly efficient for propagator
estimation. Intuitively, BCMMC can achieve greater precision than CMC when estimat-
ing probability densities of specific transitions (from one specific point (s, y) to some other
one (t, x), in [0, T] × R) because it concentrates the sampling effort on the target transi-
tion, whereas CMC distributes samples across the entire time-state space. Consequently,
BCMMC and CMC may be viewed as complementary approaches suited to different tasks:
while BCMMC is particularly appropriate for estimating propagators associated with spe-
34


cific transitions, typically being rare events (see Aguilar and Gatto (2024), Aguilar et al.
(2022)), CMC is preferable when one seeks to evaluate the propagator over broader regions
of the time-state space, especially those surrounding the most likely states. This approach
is also readily applicable to processes in which the functions A and B depend explicitly on
time, since Lamperti transforms can be further generalized to this setting (cf. e.g. Di Bello
et al. (2025)).
However, extending this approach to multidimensional processes is more
restrictive. When the volatility is matrix-valued, there is no general analogue of the one-
dimensional Lamperti transform that can map an arbitrary diffusion matrix to a prescribed
constant form. Consequently, the applicability of BCMMC to multidimensional processes
is limited to specific classes of processes for which a suitable auxiliary bridge process can
be efficiently sampled and whose measure is absolutely continuous with respect to that of
the multidimensional target process. A detailed assessment of the limitations and possible
extensions of the method in the multidimensional setting is an important direction for future
research.
We developed an improvement on both optimality (that reduces statistical errors of the
method) and discretization schemes (that reduces bias). Nevertheless, an exhaustive analysis
of these two refinements are outside the scope of this work and provide another important
direction for future works. For example, a simple enhancement of the discretization can
be achieved by using the general trapezoidal rule constructed on a non-uniform integration
lattice, whose density is adapted to the local smoothness properties of the function q to be
integrated.
