Abstract
Background: Metal implants can generate severe streaking and shading ar-
tifacts in computed tomography (CT), obscuring nearby anatomy and alter-
ing local Hounsfield-unit (HU) values. Conventional diffusion models typically
map images toward a Gaussian distribution and initialize reverse reconstruction
from Gaussian noise, requiring the target image to be recovered through iter-
ative denoising from an uninformative starting state. For CT restoration, this
formulation does not directly exploit anatomical information already present
in the metal-artifact image.
Purpose: To adapt a diffusion bridge framework for image-domain CT metal
artifact reduction (MAR) by directly connecting paired metal-artifact and metal-
free CT images. The proposed RDBM-MAR model initializes reverse recon-
struction from the metal-artifact CT image rather than Gaussian noise, aiming
to retain available anatomical information and learn a direct restoration tra-
jectory toward the metal-free target.
Methods: RDBM-MAR constructs an endpoint-conditioned diffusion bridge
between paired metal-free and metal-artifact CT images. During training, in-
termediate bridge states are generated between the two endpoints, and a con-
ditional U-Net receives the bridge state, metal-artifact image, and timestep to
estimate the metal-free CT image through an image-domain correction. The
forward bridge used 100 timesteps, while inference used 10 deterministic re-
verse steps initialized from the metal-artifact image. Training, validation, and
quantitative testing used 14,000 paired hybrid-simulated CT slices from the
AAPM CT Metal Artifact Reduction Grand Challenge. These slices were de-
rived from the DeepLesion dataset and the UCLH Stroke EIT Dataset, with
virtual metal objects and artifacts generated using CatSim in the open-source
2


XCIST toolkit. The data were divided into 12,045 training, 633 validation,
and 1,322 held-out test slices. One case from the 29-case AAPM CT-MAR
final scoring dataset was used for qualitative evaluation.
RDBM-MAR was
compared with CycleGAN, Pix2Pix, Res-SRDiff, I2I-Mamba, and I2SB using
PSNR, SSIM, MAE, and LPIPS. Statistical analysis used the Friedman test
followed by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction.
Results: RDBM-MAR achieved the highest PSNR (43.67 ± 4.56 dB) and
SSIM (0.9856 ± 0.0138) and the lowest MAE (14.74 ± 8.68 HU) among the
evaluated methods. Significant between-method differences were observed for
all four metrics (p < 0.001). Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm
correction showed significantly better PSNR, SSIM, and MAE for RDBM-MAR
than for all five comparison methods (adjusted p < 0.001). Res-SRDiff achieved
the lowest LPIPS. Difference maps and HU line-profile analysis showed reduced
artifact-related intensity errors with preservation of local anatomical structures.
The AAPM final scoring case also showed qualitative reduction of visible metal-
associated streaking.
Conclusions: RDBM-MAR provides a direct restoration trajectory from the
metal-artifact CT image toward the metal-free target while retaining anatomi-
cal information in the input. The proposed formulation achieved strong struc-
tural fidelity and HU accuracy on the held-out paired test set using 10 reverse
sampling steps, supporting diffusion bridge modeling for image-domain CT
MAR.

Introduction
Computed tomography (CT) is a cornerstone of clinical imaging, providing detailed
visualization of anatomical structures together with quantitative tissue information
through the Hounsfield unit (HU) scale. Metal implants, however, violate the as-
sumptions underlying standard reconstruction: beam hardening, photon starvation,
scatter, and the resulting inconsistencies in the projection data generate bright and
dark streaks that radiate from the metal [1]. These artifacts distort local HU values,
obscure the anatomy immediately adjacent to the implant, and propagate into down-
stream tasks such as segmentation, anatomical localization, and quantitative image
analysis. Metal artifact reduction (MAR) has therefore been studied extensively, yet
suppressing artifacts while simultaneously preserving local anatomical structure and
quantitative CT intensity remains an open problem [1, 2].
Approaches to MAR fall broadly into projection-domain and image-domain fami-
lies. Projection-domain methods, including linear interpolation [3], normalized MAR [4],
and their learning-based successors [5], correct the inconsistent measurements at their
physical origin and can therefore be highly effective, but they require access to raw
projection data together with the scanner geometry, bowtie filter, and calibration pa-
rameters used for reconstruction. Such data are proprietary and are rarely exported
in clinical practice, where the archived product is the reconstructed image; models
developed for one scanner geometry additionally transfer poorly to another. Errors
introduced during sinogram completion also propagate along entire ray paths and
can manifest as secondary artifacts remote from the implant [6], whereas corrections
applied in the image domain remain spatially local. We therefore adopt an image-
domain formulation, which is directly applicable to retrospective and clinical data for
4


which only reconstructed images are available.
Within the image domain, a network learns the mapping from artifact-corrupted
CT images to their metal-free counterparts from paired training data. Conditional
image-to-image translation models [7] achieve strong performance on paired restora-
tion tasks and have been applied directly to MAR [8], suppressing prominent streaks
and restoring the appearance of corrupted regions. Their adversarial objectives, how-
ever, are not intensity-preserving by construction, and reported failure modes include
local HU shifts, residual streaking, and over-smoothing of fine anatomical detail [8, 6].
This limitation reflects a broader tension between perceptual quality and distortion
fidelity [9], and it is consequential in MAR, where the corrected image is frequently
used for quantitative measurement.
Diffusion models address part of this problem by modeling restoration as a gradual
stochastic transformation between image distributions [10, 11], but in the conventional
formulation sampling begins from Gaussian noise and the degraded observation enters
only as conditioning information, even though that observation already carries most of
the anatomical and intensity content of the target. A more efficient alternative is to let
the residual between the paired images drive the process. We previously showed that
shifting the forward process along the residual between low- and high-resolution MRI
aligns the two distributions directly and permits high-fidelity reconstruction in as few
as four sampling steps, at a fraction of the cost of conditional diffusion [12]. Related
formulations construct an explicit stochastic bridge between the paired endpoints [13,
14], and allow the residual, where available, to modulate where noise is injected and
removed, so that perturbation concentrates on degraded regions while intact anatomy
is left largely undisturbed [14].
5


Metal artifacts differ from the natural-image degradations considered in previous
diffusion bridge studies. They are signed and non-local, correlated along ray paths
rather than confined to a compact region, and the restoration target is a quanti-
tative HU value rather than a plausible visual appearance. For CT metal artifact
reduction, we therefore use the uniform-modulation Ornstein–Uhlenbeck bridge for-
mulation. The paired clean-to-artifact difference remains in the bridge mean, while
the Gaussian perturbation is spatially uniform. This provides a direct stochastic tran-
sition between the paired metal-free and metal-artifact endpoints without residual-
dependent noise modulation.
In the proposed method, the metal-free CT image x0 and its paired metal-artifact
image µ are defined as the two endpoints of the diffusion bridge, following the nota-
tion of Wang et al. [14], in which µ denotes the degraded observation rather than the
linear attenuation coefficient. During training, intermediate bridge states are sam-
pled between the endpoints, and a conditional U-Net [15] receives the current bridge
state, the metal-artifact image, and the timestep, and estimates the corresponding
metal-free CT image. At inference, the reverse process is initialized directly from the
observed metal-artifact image and recovers the clean image along a shortened sam-
pling trajectory. Because the network predicts an image-domain correction relative
to the degraded input rather than synthesizing a complete CT image from Gaussian
noise, the restoration proceeds directly from the observed metal-artifact image toward
the metal-free target while retaining the anatomy already present in the input.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We adapt an endpoint-conditioned Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion bridge to image-
domain CT metal artifact reduction, in which the artifact-corrupted scan serves
6


as a fixed endpoint of the stochastic process and the network predicts an image-
domain correction relative to the degraded input, preserving the anatomy and
HU content already present in the scan. The contribution is the MAR-specific
instantiation and systematic evaluation, rather than the bridge formulation it-
self.
• We use the uniform-modulation bridge formulation, in which the paired clean-
to-artifact difference contributes to the bridge mean while the Gaussian pertur-
bation remains spatially uniform, avoiding residual-dependent stochastic mod-
ulation while retaining direct conditioning on the paired endpoints.
• We benchmark the proposed method against representative methods from four
distinct restoration families including adversarial (Pix2Pix [7], CycleGAN [16]),
diffusion-based (Res-SRDiff [12]), bridge-based (I2SB [13]), and state-space (I2I-
Mamba [17]), all trained and evaluated under identical data splits and prepro-
cessing on a common held-out CT-MAR test set.
• We evaluate both fidelity and perceptual quality using peak signal-to-noise ratio
(PSNR), structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [18], mean absolute error
(MAE), and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) [19], and re-
port slice-level metric distributions with paired statistical comparisons rather
than cohort means alone, so that differences between methods are assessed for
consistency as well as magnitude.
• We separate artifact suppression from quantitative intensity recovery through
localized difference maps and HU line-profile analysis, addressing a distinction
that aggregate image-quality metrics do not capture.
7


• We assess generalization beyond the simulated training distribution on clinical
CT data from the AAPM CT-MAR benchmark [20, 21].

Materials and methods
2.1
Data Preparation
The proposed model was developed and evaluated on the paired image-domain CT
data released by the AAPM CT Metal Artifact Reduction (CT-MAR) grand chal-
lenge [20, 22].
The challenge database was constructed with a hybrid simulation
framework [21] that inserts virtual metal objects into clinical CT images drawn from
DeepLesion [23] and the UCLH Stroke EIT dataset [24], covering head and body
anatomy.
Artifact-corrupted projections and their reconstructions were generated
with the CatSim simulator in the open-source XCIST toolkit [25], which models
quantum noise, beam hardening, and scatter.
Each sample therefore comprises a
metal-artifact CT image and its spatially registered metal-free reference, enabling
supervised training in the image domain.
All images were processed using the same preprocessing procedure. CT values
were clipped to the range of [−1000, 2500] HU to standardize the intensity range and
retain diagnostically relevant tissue information, followed by linear normalization to
[0, 1]. The identical normalization procedure was applied to both the metal-artifact
input images and their corresponding metal-free references. No spatial resizing was
performed, and all images were retained at their native 512 × 512 resolution.
A total of 12,045 paired samples were used for training, while 633 paired samples
were allocated for validation. A separate held-out test set containing 1,322 paired
8


samples was reserved exclusively for final model evaluation and was not used during
model training or model selection. The pairing between each metal-artifact image
and its corresponding metal-free reference was strictly maintained throughout data
partitioning and evaluation.
For qualitative assessment, one clinical case from the AAPM CT-MAR scoring
set was additionally evaluated [20, 21]. This case contained a metal implant, had
no metal-free ground truth, and was not used during training. Metal regions were
delineated from the unclipped HU image by thresholding at 3000 HU and are overlaid
in red on all displayed images; the resulting mask was used for visualization only and
was never supplied to the model during training or inference.
2.2
CT Metal Artifact Diffusion Bridge
For each paired CT sample, the metal-free image is denoted by x0 and the correspond-
ing metal-artifact image by µ. The forward process establishes a stochastic transition
from the metal-free image to its degraded counterpart, whereas the reverse process
reconstructs the metal-free image beginning from the observed metal-artifact CT.
We adopt the generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck bridge [26], in which Doob’s h-
transform conditions a mean-reverting OU process on the fixed terminal state xT = µ,
eliminating the terminal variance and yielding a point-to-point mapping between the
paired images. This formulation is the uniform-modulation case of the generalized
bridge family described by Wang et al. [14]: the stochastic modulation term is held at
unity, so the Gaussian perturbation is spatially uniform rather than residual-weighted.

2.3
Implementation Details
The forward bridge was discretized into T = 100 timesteps with bridge variance λ =
10−4. At each training iteration, a timestep t was drawn uniformly from {0, . . . , T −1}
and Gaussian noise ϵ ∼N(0, I) was used to construct the corresponding intermediate
bridge state via Eq. (2). Because a single conditional network is shared across all
timesteps, this sampling exposes the same weights to the full range of intermediate
corruption levels, and the same trained model is applied at every reverse stage.
13


The network was trained using direct clean-image supervision. Specifically, the
final prediction was parameterized as an additive image-domain correction relative to
the observed metal-artifact image,
ˆx0 = µ + ∆ϕ(xt, µ, t),
(8)
and optimized using an L1 reconstruction objective,
L1 = ∥ˆx0 −x0∥1 .
(9)
The model was optimized using Adam [27] with a learning rate of 8×10−5 and ex-
ponential decay coefficients (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.99). Training was performed for 120,000
iterations using a batch size of 10. Gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0
was applied during optimization. An exponential moving average of the model pa-
rameters was maintained with a decay factor of 0.995 and used for validation and
inference.
At inference the schedule was reduced to K = 10 reverse steps, selected by uniform
spacing from the training discretization.
Reconstruction was initialized from the
observed metal-artifact image and no stochastic term was injected, so inference is
deterministic for a fixed input and parameter set. All slices were processed at their
native 512 × 512 resolution, and outputs were mapped back to HU by inverting the
normalization described in Section 2.1 before metric computation.

2.4
Comparison Methods
The proposed diffusion bridge model was evaluated against five representative image
restoration approaches: Pix2Pix [7], CycleGAN [16], Res-SRDiff [12], I2I-Mamba [17],
and I2SB [13]. These methods were selected to represent different image-to-image
restoration paradigms and to provide comparisons with both conventional and more
recent deep-learning approaches.
Pix2Pix provides a supervised conditional generative adversarial network baseline
that directly learns a mapping between paired source and target images. CycleGAN
represents an unpaired adversarial translation framework and therefore does not re-
quire one-to-one correspondence between source and target images during optimiza-
tion. Res-SRDiff provides a residual-shifting diffusion-based restoration approach.
I2I-Mamba uses selective state-space modeling for image-to-image transformation,
while I2SB formulates paired image translation as a Schr¨odinger bridge between the
source and target distributions. Together, these models cover adversarial, diffusion-
based, state-space-based, and bridge-based image restoration strategies.
For all comparison methods, the metal-artifact CT images were used as the source
images and the corresponding metal-free CT images as the restoration targets. The
same training, validation, and held-out test partitions were used across all methods,
together with identical HU clipping, intensity normalization, and image resolution.
For supervised approaches, the original artifact–metal-free pairing was preserved dur-
ing training. CycleGAN used the same source and target training sets but did not
use their paired correspondence, consistent with its original unpaired training formu-
lation.
Each comparison model was adapted only as required to accommodate the single-
15


channel 512 × 512 CT data and the artifact-to-metal-free restoration task while re-
taining its original learning formulation as closely as possible. Model selection was
performed using the corresponding validation set, and final quantitative evaluation
was conducted exclusively on the same 1,322 held-out test slices. The identical metal-
free reference images and evaluation procedure were used for all methods to ensure
direct slice-level comparison.
2.5
Quantitative and Statistical Analysis
Quantitative reconstruction performance was evaluated using PSNR, SSIM, MAE,
and learned LPIPS. Higher PSNR and SSIM values indicate better performance,
whereas lower MAE and LPIPS values indicate better performance.
Each metric was calculated independently for every held-out test slice using the
corresponding metal-free CT image as the reference. PSNR and SSIM were com-
puted using the same normalized intensity representation for all evaluated methods.
For MAE calculation, reconstructed and reference images were mapped back to the
original HU scale, and MAE was therefore reported in HU. For LPIPS evaluation, the
single-channel CT images were replicated across three channels and mapped to the
input range required by the pretrained LPIPS network. The same evaluation pipeline
was applied to all reconstruction methods. Quantitative results were summarized as
mean ± standard deviation, and slice-level metric distributions were visualized using
boxplots.
Statistical comparisons were performed using nonparametric paired analyses. For
each image-quality metric, differences among the six reconstruction methods were
first assessed using the Friedman test for repeated measures, with each held-out test
16


slice treated as a matched observation across methods. The metal-artifact input was
not included in the Friedman analysis. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) was
calculated to quantify the effect size of the overall between-model differences.
Following a significant Friedman test, pairwise comparisons between the proposed
method and each of the five comparison models were performed using the Wilcoxon
signed-rank test on the paired slice-level metric values. Holm correction was applied
separately within each image-quality metric to account for multiple comparisons. An
adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Median paired differences
were additionally reported for the closest-performing comparison method to describe
the magnitude and direction of the observed differences.

The paired statistical analysis further supported these findings. The Friedman tests
showed significant differences among the evaluated methods for all four metrics, with
Kendall’s W values ranging from 0.723 to 0.905. Pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
with Holm correction showed that the proposed model significantly outperformed all
five comparison methods in PSNR, SSIM, and MAE. These results indicate that the
observed improvements were consistent across the paired test slices rather than being
driven only by differences in average performance.
The qualitative results were consistent with the quantitative evaluation. In both
the representative head and thoracic CT examples, the proposed model substantially
reduced metal-induced streak artifacts while preserving the surrounding anatomical
structures. The corresponding difference maps showed smaller residual HU deviations
in artifact-affected regions. The HU line-profile analysis further showed that the re-
24


constructed CT values preserved the overall intensity trend of the metal-free reference
with reduced local fluctuations. Together with the lower MAE observed across the
full test set, these findings support the quantitative reliability of HU recovery by the
proposed model and suggest that artifact-related intensity errors can be reduced with
lower risks of altering the underlying anatomical information.
The ranking of the methods differed for LPIPS. Res-SRDiff achieved the lowest
LPIPS, followed by I2SB, whereas the proposed model achieved the best performance
in PSNR, SSIM, and MAE. Pairwise testing similarly showed that the proposed model
had lower LPIPS than CycleGAN, Pix2Pix, and I2I-Mamba, but higher LPIPS than
Res-SRDiff and I2SB. This difference indicates that the similarity of the learned per-
ceptual features and the accuracy of CT intensity do not necessarily rank the re-
construction methods in the same order, consistent with the perception–distortion
trade-off in image restoration [9]. In the reduction of CT metal artifacts, this distinc-
tion is important because accurate recovery of HU values and anatomical structures
is required in addition to visual similarity. The lower MAE of the proposed method
therefore provides complementary information to the LPIPS results. Unlike natural-
image restoration, CT reconstruction requires preservation not only of visual appear-
ance but also of quantitatively meaningful image intensities. HU values carry crucial
physical information that is used in quantitative image interpretation and clinical
analysis. In this context, the lower MAE achieved by the proposed model indicates
improved recovery of CT intensity values and complements the information provided
by perceptual metrics such as LPIPS. The simultaneous improvement in PSNR, SSIM,
and MAE therefore suggests that the proposed method reduces artifact-related errors
while maintaining both structural and quantitative fidelity to the metal-free reference.
25


The observed performance may be related to the formulation of the restoration
process. Unlike conventional diffusion approaches that initialize reverse reconstruc-
tion from an independent Gaussian sample, the diffusion bridge starts directly from
the observed metal-artifact CT image.
As a result, anatomical information that
remains present in the degraded observation is retained at the beginning of recon-
struction rather than being regenerated from noise. The conditional U-Net further
predicts an image-domain correction relative to the metal-artifact image, focusing the
reconstruction on the differences between the degraded observation and the metal-
free target. The bridge formulation therefore provides a direct restoration trajectory
between the paired artifact and metal-free endpoints. In addition, the shortened de-
terministic reverse trajectory allows reconstruction to be performed with a limited
number of network evaluations. Although the contribution of each component was not
separately evaluated in the current study, these design characteristics may contribute
to the strong intensity and structural fidelity observed in the results.
The clinical CT example from the AAPM CT-MAR scoring dataset provided an
additional qualitative evaluation beyond the paired benchmark data. The model re-
duced visible streak artifacts surrounding the metal regions and improved the visibility
of nearby anatomical structures. However, a metal-free reference was not available for
this case, and the clinical result should therefore be interpreted qualitatively rather
than as a quantitative validation of performance on real metal artifacts.
Several limitations remain.
The quantitative evaluation was performed using
paired benchmark data generated through the AAPM CT-MAR simulation frame-
work, and performance on clinical CT images may differ because of variations in
scanner configuration, implant composition, anatomy, and artifact severity. The cur-
26


rent model also processes each CT slice independently and does not use information
from adjacent slices or three-dimensional anatomical continuity.
In addition, the
clinical evaluation was limited to a single scoring case without a metal-free refer-
ence. Future work will therefore include evaluation on a larger range of clinical CT
images and implant types, reader-based assessment, and extension of the framework
to adjacent-slice or three-dimensional reconstruction. Metal masks and projection-
domain measurements could also be incorporated as additional conditioning informa-
tion. Finally, alternative training objectives may help improve perceptual similarity
while preserving the HU accuracy and structural fidelity achieved by the current
model.
