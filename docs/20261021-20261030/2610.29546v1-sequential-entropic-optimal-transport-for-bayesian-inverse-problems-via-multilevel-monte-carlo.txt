Abstract
We consider the problem of Bayesian estimation associated to inverse problems governed by
partial differential equations (PDEs). In most cases of practical interest, one must use numerical
solvers to implement computational methods based on Monte Carlo methods. This inevitably
leads to an adaptation of multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC) methods to reduce computational
efforts to achieve a pre-specified mean square error (MSE). MLMC is based on sampling coupled
versions of Bayesian posteriors associated to increasingly precise numerical solves of the PDE.
In this article we present a novel method to implement MLMC based upon sequential entropic
optimal transport couplings learned with light Schrödinger bridges, and correct the transport
with Metropolis–Hastings steps that preserve the pairing.
We bound the mean square error
associated to a version of our new estimation method and show, for an elliptic inverse problem,
that to achieve a MSE of O(δ2) (δ > 0) one can have an optimal cost of O(δ−2). The results
feature explicit bounds on the effect of the fitted transport and of the Metropolis–Hastings
correction. From a numerical perspective, on elliptic, Darcy-flow and Navier–Stokes problems,
the method reduces the variance of the most precise solver by a factor of five to ten and is faster
than multilevel sequential Monte Carlo [6] at tight accuracies.
Keywords: Entropic Optimal Transport, Light Schrödinger Bridges, Bayesian Inverse Prob-
lems, Multilevel Monte Carlo.

Introduction
Inverse problems and their Bayesian counterparts are found in a large class of practical applications,
which include physics, engineering, finance, and applied mathematics; see e.g. [40] and the references
therein. In many cases, these types of models are related to partial differential equations (PDEs),
which require numerical solution, and, in addition, the associated Bayesian estimation problem relates
to a posterior density that can only be computed point-wise and with numerical error associated to
the PDE solver. This naturally means that one must resort to computational methods based on,
for example, Markov chain simulation, for which there have been numerous contributions; see for
example [5, 14, 25, 6].
The intention of this article is to introduce a new methodology, using machine learning methods,
which can improve in terms of accuracy and speed, when related to these afore-mentioned Markov
chain methods. Our approach will be based on entropic optimal transport and to that end, we give
some mathematical background to provide context going forward.

1.3
Related work, contributions and paper structure
The main idea of this article is to use EOT to sample couplings of πl and πl−1 within the context
of Multilevel Monte Carlo [9, 16, 17, 19] (MLMC). MLMC writes πL(φ) as a telescoping sum of
corrections between consecutive levels and estimates each one using coupled samples. The utility
2


of doing so is that for sufficiently good couplings one gains a variance reduction and choosing the
number of simulated samples appropriately, one can reduce the computational cost to achieve a mean
square error (MSE), versus just considering sampling from πL say. The ‘good couplings’ we mention
above will be obtained by using EOT.
In the context of MLMC, and for Bayesian inverse problems, couplings have been built with
Markov chains [14, 21] or with sequential Monte Carlo samplers [12], as in the multilevel sequential
Monte Carlo (MLSMC) method of [6], later extended with likelihood-informed proposals [7] and
with tempering [27]. Sequential Monte Carlo methods of this type have been applied, for instance,
to inference for the Navier–Stokes equations [25], Lagrangian data assimilation with unknown ob-
servation locations [38] and, more recently, zeroth-order stochastic bilevel optimization [8]. Instead
of reweighting samples of one distribution to represent another, optimal transport moves samples,
through a deterministic map or a Markov kernel that pushes the first distribution onto the second;
see [30] for an introduction. Transport maps have been used to sample posteriors directly [15], also
as compositions of maps between consecutive tempered densities [10, 41], to accelerate Markov chain
Monte Carlo [35, 36], and to combine annealing with multi-fidelity models [39]. In multilevel estima-
tors they have been used to couple the levels [23, 32], for instance by pushing a common reference
sample through a map fitted to each level. In sequential Monte Carlo, normalizing flows [2, 31], con-
trolled transitions [20] and Schrödinger bridges [4] have been learned between consecutive tempered
distributions, with importance weights to correct the transport.
Rather than a deterministic map, entropic optimal transport [28, 33] gives a coupling of two
distributions: the joint law with the prescribed marginals that minimizes the expected squared
distance between the coupled points plus an entropy penalty. Its conditional laws act as a stochastic
transport map, and the coupled pairs are close when the two distributions are close. The coupling is
computed by Sinkhorn iterations between empirical measures [11, 37], whose stability is studied in
[13]. The light Schrödinger bridge [18, 26] parametrizes the potential by a Gaussian mixture, which
makes the coupling cheap to fit from samples and exact to simulate.
In this article we present two distinct methods for implementing MLMC via EOT. In the first,
we use a light Schrödinger bridge to learn the EOT between each πl and πl−1 and combine this with
a type of particle simulation method. In the second, this approach is augmented with Metropolis-
Hastings steps.
We then provide a mathematical analysis of our approach in terms of an MSE
analysis. We bound the MSE associated to a version of our new estimation method and show, for
an elliptic inverse problem, that to achieve a MSE of O(δ2) (δ > 0) one can have an optimal cost
of O(δ−2), depending on the cost of the solver. The results feature explicit bounds on the effect of
the fitted transport and of the Metropolis–Hastings correction. We then consider three numerical
examples associate to elliptic, Darcy-flow and Navier–Stokes problems. We show that the second
method reduces the variance of the most precise solver by a factor of five to ten and is faster than
multilevel sequential Monte Carlo [6] at tight accuracies.
This article is structured as follows. In section 2 our methodology is detailed. In Section 3 we
present our mathematical results, with some proofs deferred to the appendix. Section 4 details our
numerical results.

4
Numerical Experiments
Section 3 covers exact couplings and, through crude bounds, the fitted kernels with and without
the Metropolis–Hastings correction. We now assess them numerically, comparing TransportMH with
the multilevel sequential Monte Carlo (MLSMC) sampler of [6] on three Bayesian inverse problems:
the one-dimensional elliptic problem of [6], with a smaller observation noise than in that paper; a
two-dimensional Darcy-flow problem; and, in the spirit of [25], the recovery of the initial vorticity
of a two-dimensional Navier–Stokes flow from noisy point observations at later times. The first two
have finite-element forward maps; the third is nonlinear and time dependent. In all three the first
transition is hard and the later ones are easy: the ESS fraction of γ1/γ0 on a π0 cloud is 3%, 1.2%
and 1.8%, whereas at the second transition it is already about 60% and 73% in test cases 2 and 3.
All computations are in Python (NumPy/SciPy for the PDE solves, PyTorch for the LightSB fits)
on a single CPU thread of an Apple M4 Pro laptop. Implementation details, the sample sizes, the
computation of the reference values and a breakdown of the computing time are in Appendix B.

Where the gain comes from.
Figure 2 shows that the level-1 correction dominates the variance
for all methods, and that TransportMH reduces its variance by factors of about 6, 5 and 10 in
test cases 1–3 (standard deviations 0.0037, 0.0040 and 0.0044 against 0.0090, 0.0089 and 0.0139 for
MLSMC-pCN). In MLSMC the level-1 increment is an importance-sampling average over the N0
particles at an ESS of a few percent, computed before the mutation, so extra sweeps cannot reduce
it: the level-1 curves of the two MLSMC variants coincide, and the sweeps only help from level 2
on, where MLSMC-pCN with ESS sweeps has the smaller corrections. In TransportMH the level-1
correction is a paired difference along the transport, whose variance is controlled by the transport
cost rather than by the ESS.
Computing time.
TransportMH and MLSMC-pCN with ESS sweeps make almost the same num-
ber of PDE solves, most of them in the 30 sweeps at the first transition, but the extra solves of
TransportMH, at the transported particles and in the MH sweeps at the final levels, sit where solves
are most expensive (Appendix B.4). The LightSB fits cost about 3 s per run whatever the Nl: a fifth
of the total in test case 1, where solves are cheap, and 2–2.5% in test cases 2 and 3. At fine levels,
where el ≈1, the MH sweeps move little (acceptance 94–99%) but each adds about as much noise to
Xi
l −Xi
l−1 as the transport, so three sweeps multiply the variance of the paired differences by about
four. Skipping them there would bring the L = 6 run of test case 2 from 119 s to roughly 60–65 s;
we have not tested its effect on the bias.
Limitations.
Two features of TransportMH call for comment.
(a) Fine-level variances. The level-l correction standard deviation of TransportMH levels off at
about 3×10−4 in test case 1 (levels 7–9), while those of the MLSMC variants keep decreasing, to

5
Discussion
Learned entropic transport between consecutive posteriors, corrected by Metropolis–Hastings steps
that keep the pairing, helps most at the coarse transitions where the incremental weights degenerate;
at fine levels the importance-sampling corrections of MLSMC are already accurate. This suggests
a hybrid scheme, with transport only where the effective sample size is low and fewer or no fine-
level sweeps, together with an adaptive choice of εl and of the number of mixture components.
On the theoretical side, the analysis covers exact couplings and bounds the effect of the fitted
16


kernels only crudely; a sharper treatment requires bounds on the error of the stochastic gradient fit,
on the approximation of the oracle potential by Gaussian mixtures, and on the contraction of the
preconditioned Crank–Nicolson kernel used in practice. Finally, higher-dimensional parameters, such
as discretized random fields, would call for more structured potentials, for instance with low-rank
covariances.
