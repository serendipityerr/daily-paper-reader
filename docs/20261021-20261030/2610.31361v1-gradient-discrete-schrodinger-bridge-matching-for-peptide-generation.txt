GRADIENT DISCRETE SCHR¨ODINGER BRIDGE MATCHING FOR
PEPTIDE GENERATION
ABDALLAH BENABDALLAH AND MOHSEN DLALA
Abstract. The optimal control of a discrete Schr¨odinger bridge is a discrete gra-
dient, a difference of vertex potentials, but recent models such as MadSBM instead
learn a free control on the edges by cross-entropy. We prove that this leaves the ac-
tion uncontrolled: cross-entropy identifies the control only up to a state-dependent
shift (a gauge), and the action is KL(r∥p) plus a gauge term the loss never constrains.
We propose GDSBM, which writes the control as a difference of vertex potentials
and adds the action to the loss; pinning the gauge costs no expressiveness and makes
the action a property of the model, so the penalty acts on the model and not on
the gauge. On peptide generation our MadSBM reimplementation has a free-gauge
action of order 108 for a gauge-fixed value of 6.5, against 1.5 for GDSBM, with no
quality loss that three seeds can detect. The gradient form also fits guidance, which
becomes the addition of a potential: guided toward a net charge of +3 at pH 7,
GDSBM is closer than MadSBM, over three seeds, to its exact tempered target in
total variation at every guidance strength (at β = 1, 81% of samples in the target
window against 74%, for a target of 97%); we claim no statistical significance.
1. Introduction
Peptides occupy the therapeutic space between small molecules and antibodies: spe-
cific enough to hit flat protein surfaces, small enough to manufacture chemically [53].
Their discovery still relies on screening large libraries and keeping the rare hits, whereas
generative models promise to invert the pipeline and sample candidates conditioned on
the desired function [2, 81, 73]. The promise is already concrete for proteins—structure
prediction [37, 1], language models [60, 46, 52, 31], structure- and sequence-level de-
sign [22, 35]—and for antimicrobial peptides, where generative models have produced
laboratory-tested candidates [21, 72] and biologically informed generators are emerg-
ing [68]. Making it quantitative requires two things current discrete generators do not
simultaneously offer: a principled training objective, and conditioning that is exact
rather than heuristic.
Continuous-state models dominate protein structure generation [32, 69, 48, 3, 83, 45,
12, 81]; on sequences the generators of choice are discrete diffusions and flows [6, 34,
14, 50, 61, 65, 15, 71], now at language-model scale [55] and instantiated for proteins
[2, 79, 47]. Closest to this work is Schr¨odinger-bridge matching, first continuous [23,

MadSBM [29] is, to our knowledge, the first discrete bridge model brought to the
scale of biological sequences: a controlled jump process on the amino-acid edit graph,
anchored to a frozen protein language model [46], with the forward–backward iterations
[74] that are hard to scale to |V|L states replaced by a token-wise cross-entropy. This
is a genuine advance and our starting point. Its price, however, is structural, and it is
precisely what makes a bridge a bridge. The control is not an h-transform of the ESM-2
reference: it is a free field uθ(x, y, t) on the edges which, by MadSBM’s own consistency
theorem, cross-entropy recovers only up to an additive c(x, t), whereas the optimum is
a difference of vertex values, u∗(x, y, t) = log h(y, t) −log h(x, t). The loss is not the
action: cross-entropy is minimized by every representative of the class u + c, whose
actions differ without bound (Section 4), so the reported action is a statement about
the representative the optimizer happens to produce rather than about the model.
Conditioning, finally, is a logit tilt known to be biased for tempered targets [42, 78], a
bias usually asserted and left unmeasured. MadSBM keeps the construction and the
name of the Schr¨odinger bridge but, relative to the ESM-2 reference, neither its control
class nor its objective. Figure 1 contrasts the three objects.
Observation, and a gauge. Doob’s h-transform states that the optimal bridge control
is a difference of vertex functions [28, 43].
We take this seriously three times: for
parameterization (learn a vertex potential, not an edge field), for conditioning (design
objectives are vertex functions, and composing h-transforms adds potentials), and for

functions). A fourth observation concerns not what the control should be but what
training identifies. At each state the conditional law of the next token is a softmax
of reference logits plus control, and a softmax is invariant to adding a function of
the current state; a control learned by cross-entropy is therefore identified only up to

The vertex form of the optimum is the natural gauge fixing: it pins the term the softmax

This changes how the action of a bridge model must be reported, and what it means
to regularize it.
Contributions. Three main results, one per section, with all proofs in the appendix.
• The action of a free control measures its gauge (C1, Theorem 1). The
per-state action splits exactly into KL(r∥pθ), a function of the learned conditional
alone, and a gauge excess that cross-entropy never constrains and that is unbounded
along the orbit of a model; at the forward Girsanov kernel the gauge-fixed value is
instead bounded and invertible in KL(pθ∥r). Measured, a trained free field reports
108 for a gauge-fixed 6.5.
• Vertex-potential control and the exact effect of the penalty (C2, Theo-
rem 2). We parameterize uθ(x, y, t) = vθ(y, t) −vθ(x, t), the form of the optimum,
from a zero-initialized energy head. This pins the gauge without restricting the rep-
resentable conditionals, so at λ = 0 the model is functionally identical to MadSBM
[29]; the gauge term becomes an anchor on the current token; and the penalty has
an exact effect, a convex shrinkage of the data posterior towards the reference with
weight λνV /(1 + λνV ).
• Conditioning is algebra on potentials, and its bias is measurable (C3,
Theorem 3). A tilt is an h-transform whose potential adds to the trained one, so
a multi-objective sweep needs no retraining; for any scalar statistic the tempered
target’s marginal is closed-form, with no product structure assumed; and a particle
system with telescoping weights reaches it. On peptides the net charge is exactly
computable, turning the bias of the myopic guided sampler into a measurement
against an explicit sampling floor.
What we do not claim. No wet-lab validation and no state-of-the-art claim: we compare
to our own MadSBM reimplementation at matched compute and seeds, reporting effect
sizes and sign consistency, not significance.
We do not claim that vθ is a globally
consistent potential (consistency across states is learned, not enforced), that the vertex
form helps by itself (at λ = 0 the two models coincide) or beyond the penalty (the
2 × 2 ablation has one seed, 300 steps), or that we minimize the Schr¨odinger action.
Multi-objective guidance and the particle system are stated, not evaluated.

2. Related Work
Our question is narrow: for sequence generation on a discrete state space, should
the control of a Schr¨odinger bridge be a free field on the edges of the sequence graph,
or an exact discrete gradient of a vertex potential?
Bridges, and potentials versus fields. The Schr¨odinger problem [63, 43] is the en-
tropic regularisation of dynamical optimal transport [9, 16, 20]; continuous-state neu-
ral solvers differ in which fixed-point iteration they discretise [23, 64, 75, 49].
In
Rd the optimal control is a gradient, u∗= ∇log h, and methods that exploit this—
scalar-potential transport [54], adjoint and stochastic-optimal-control matching [25,
26], energy-based parametrisations [7]—gain by removing a rotational component that
carries no transport. On graphs the geometry of entropy-driven dynamics is well un-
derstood [51, 27, 17] and is only now becoming a learning algorithm [58], but on a
simplex with (|V| + 1)L vertices neither the metric nor the free energy is computable,
so we work with the control of a jump process and the discrete gradient v(y) −v(x)
along an edge.
Discrete generation and discrete bridges. Discrete diffusion [6, 14], score-entropy mod-
els [50], masked diffusion [61, 65] and discrete flow matching [15] learn the reversal of
a reference jump process; discrete bridge formulations [38, 40, 5, 74] place a control on
its rates. MadSBM [29] does so for masked sequences with an ESM-2 reference [46],
trained by cross-entropy; it is our direct baseline. All learn general rate structures,
whereas we impose uθ(x, y, t) = vθ(y, t) −vθ(x, t), which removes cycle inconsistencies
by construction, and we make the associated action explicit and use it to audit the
learned bridge.
Guidance, reward optimisation, and the test domain. Function-conditioned design has
been attacked as model-based optimisation and reinforcement learning [13, 4, 70, 66,
39, 10, 36, 57], as inference-time guidance [24, 33, 18, 30, 56, 62, 59, 44, 82, 76, 67],
and as reward fine-tuning [11, 77, 25, 80], all presuming the generator is a well-defined
object whose conditional laws can be tilted; whether the control of a bridge is such an
object is our question. The exact twist, h(x, t) = E[eφ(XT ) | Xt = x], is intractable here
and practical surrogates are biased [42]; we apply the same h-transform viewpoint to
a control that is already a vertex potential, so the twist adds a second potential to the
first. We evaluate on peptides, as MadSBM does, for their natural reference process
and exactly computable objectives; our comparison is to MadSBM alone, at matched
corpus, compute and seeds.

head gives ˜vθ, and the loss (13) is computed with AΨ on uθ = ¯∇˜vθ. Only the head

Sampling. We use MadSBM’s sampler unchanged, with the same engineering constants,
for both models: only the control field differs, so the comparison bears on a single
object, the control class and the penalty. Algorithm 2 starts from the fully masked
sequence and, at each of N steps, uses the control to decide which masked positions
jump and which amino acid they receive. Two features bear on what the audit of
Section 7.1 measures. The jump probability uses the control damped by a rate scale

on the same scale; in particular the jump schedule, unlike the token kernel, is not
gauge-invariant. And the candidate set is restricted to the 20 amino acids after the
nucleus filter, so the tokens carrying the mass 1−νV of Theorem 2(c) are never emitted.

7. Experiments
The experiments answer three questions, in order. Is the action of a cross-entropy-
trained control a statement about transport, or about the gauge (Section 7.1)? Does
the vertex form change the model when the penalty is off, and what does the penalty
do when the form is free (Section 7.2)? And does the regularised model pay for it
in sequence quality (Section 7.3)? Section 7.4 reports the conditioning measurement.
Deviations and threats to validity are listed in Appendix G, and full tables in Appen-
dix H.
Setup. MadSBM (reimpl.) is the free edge field trained by cross-entropy alone (λ = 0);
GDSBM (ours) is the vertex-potential control of Definition 1 trained with (13) at
λ = 0.1. They share the frozen ESM-2 650M backbone [46], the AdaLN head (≈45M
trainable parameters), corpus, optimiser, schedule (127 000 steps) and sampler, so they
differ in exactly two places: the control class and the penalty. The dataset of [29] is
not public, so we rebuild a corpus from UniProt (reviewed, 5 ≤L ≤50, strict 20-
letter alphabet) split 90/5/5 at seed 42: all comparisons are internal to our two arms.
We train three seeds per arm (42, 43, 44) and sample at N ∈{32, 64, 128}, generating
20 sequences at each length L ∈{5, . . . , 50} per (arm, seed, N), i.e. 920 sequences
and 16 560 in all; pairs are always at equal seed and N. We report pseudo-perplexity
under ESM-2, mean pLDDT from ESMFold rescaled to [0, 100], the fraction of unique
sequences (Appendix G), and the action in three conventions—raw, vertex gauge, and
gauge-fixed, the minimum over the gauge class, equal to KL(r∥p) by Theorem 1—plus
its forward-kernel counterpart.

8. Conclusion
A discrete bridge trained by cross-entropy leaves one degree of freedom per state
unconstrained, and the action it reports lives almost entirely there. Theorem 1 makes
this exact and gives the comparable, gauge-fixed quantity; Theorem 2 pins the gauge
by the form of the Doob optimum, at no cost in representable conditionals, and turns
an action penalty into a convex shrinkage towards the reference; Theorem 3 makes
conditioning addition of potentials and replaces an asserted bias by a measured one.
Two questions remain open (Appendix I): a per-state regulariser is not yet a path-
space action, and a locally read potential is not yet a global one. We plan to study the
geometry of the control potential through the Onsager-type and entropy-dissipation
structure of finite-state Schr¨odinger bridges [8].

Appendix A. Deferred remarks
This appendix collects the remarks referenced from the main text.
Remark 3 (The reported action is not a property of the model). Cross-entropy identifies
a control only up to the gauge, because the softmax cannot see it, yet the action is
not gauge-invariant. The number a model reports as its action is therefore the sum
of a genuine property of the model, the divergence ν KL(r∥p), and a term that by
Theorem 1(b) can be made arbitrarily large without altering the token kernel at any
state. Any comparison of raw actions is thus void, including one in our own favour: two
parameterisations that agree as models to eight digits report raw actions differing by

a factor of 2.7, and a trained free field reports 108 for a gauge-fixed value of 6.35–6.72
(Section 7.1). The two directions are moreover not symmetric: h(s) ∼s for large s and
h(s) ∼log(1/s) for small s, so inflating the action is exponentially cheap in the shift
while deflating it is only logarithmic. The vertex form of Section 5 does not remove
the second term; it pins it, by imposing u(xi) = 0 at the current token. That term
becomes the anchor h(r(xi)/p(xi)), a function of the learned conditional alone, so the
action is again a property of the model and a penalty acts on the conditional rather
than on the gauge.
Remark 4 (Two kernels, two scales). Theorem 1(c) is the same computation at the
forward kernel a of Fact 1. The gauge-fixed forward action is bounded by the total
reference rate whatever the divergence, whereas the gauge-fixed reverse action KL(r∥p)
is unbounded; the two kernels measure two directions of divergence, not two scales of
the same one. We use the closed form of (c) to audit the same trained models at the
forward kernel (Section 7.1); the training penalty uses Ψ.
Remark 5 (What the penalty is). Theorem 2(c) says that, state by state, the penalised
minimiser is a convex shrinkage of the data posterior towards the amino-acid refer-
ence of the language model, with weight w; the restriction to V is what the sampler
uses (non-amino-acid tokens are excluded). For λ = 0.1 and νV ≃0.93, w ≃8.5%.
The penalty therefore acts as an explicit, measurable regulariser towards the protein-
language-model prior ESM-2, of the form of a mixture (q + λr)/(1 + λνV ); this is the
sense in which it lowers the action, and we do not interpret it further as a path-space
optimality statement. It is a population statement for an expressive class: a finite
network does not attain (15). We make no claim that (13) is a path-space entropy:
AΨ is a per-state functional, evaluated along noised data states.
Remark 6 (Geometry, and what is not claimed). The state space X = (V ∪{m})L
with the edit edges is the Cartesian product of L stars K1,|V | centred at the mask; it
has |X| = (|V | + 1)L vertices and |E| = L|V |(|V | + 1)L−1 edges. A global potential
v : X →R yields an edge field v(y) −v(x) with zero circulation on every closed walk.
Since each star is a tree, the cycle space of the product is generated by the squares,
so zero circulation reduces to a commutation condition: filling position i with a then
position j with b must accumulate the same control as the reverse order. A free edge
field has |E| −|X| + 1 additional rotational degrees of freedom.
The energy head,
however, reads the potentials of the neighbours from the current state: consistency
across states is learned, not enforced, and we do not claim that vθ is a global potential.
What Theorem 2(a) gives is the gauge pinning at each state, not the curl-free condition.
Remark 7 (Scope of the conditioning claims). Theorem 3 is a statement about the
structure of the conditioned model: a tilt is an h-transform, it stays in the vertex class,
rewards compose, the target of a statistic is closed-form, and a particle system reaches
it. What we measure in Section 7.4 is the single-objective myopic sampler against
the closed-form marginal of part (b); the particle system is stated but not evaluated
here, and neither is multi-objective guidance or the hydropathy statistic, for which a
reliable estimate of ˆp would require far more than n0 = 300 unguided samples. In the

From a locally read potential to a global one. The energy head reads the potentials of
the neighbours from the current state, so nothing forces the learned field to be curl-
free on the squares of the edit graph (Remark 6). A penalty on the circulation around
squares is computable in closed form on a minibatch and would make the control an
exact discrete gradient; whether this changes the generated distribution is an empirical
question we have not settled. Related to it, a bridge whose reference is the trained
conditional rather than the frozen language model would make the whole construction
self-consistent, at the cost of an outer iteration.
Geometry of the control potential. We read the potential locally, as a vector on the
neighbours of the current state, and make no use of the geometry of the sequence graph.
A natural next step is to equip the edit graph with the Onsager-type structure that
governs entropy dissipation for finite-state Schr¨odinger bridges [8], and to ask whether
a potential read in that geometry, rather than by a state-wise head, yields a control
that is globally consistent and whose action carries a dissipation interpretation. This
would address the two questions above at once.
