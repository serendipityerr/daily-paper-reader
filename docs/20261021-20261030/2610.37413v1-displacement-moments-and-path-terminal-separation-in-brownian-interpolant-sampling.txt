Displacement Moments and Path–Terminal Separation in Brownian
Interpolant Sampling
Zhong Han 1
Abstract
Brownian stochastic interpolants connect paired
endpoint samples, yet their sampling error can
depend on displacement moments even when
neither endpoint has a ﬁrst moment. For arbi-
trary Borel couplings, we prove early-stopped,
target-to-output KL guarantees for standard Eu-
ler sampling from the true source.
A fourth
displacement moment gives an explicit O(N −1)
bound; a second moment sufﬁces for ﬁxed-
law convergence as drift error vanishes.
For
one ﬁxed coupling with no displacement mo-
ment above two, original bridge-path KL stays
of order one, comparison-path KL is of order
1/ log N, and terminal KL is at most C/N 2 on
the same grid. The path decomposition identiﬁes
the information about the source discarded by
Markov projection. When the original bridge is
Markov, current-state Euler sampling instead has
a second-moment O((M2 + d)/N) bound; ﬁnite-
entropy Schrödinger bridges for the probability
Brownian reference are included. We also con-
struct a current-state learner with expected out-
put KL consistency from iid endpoint pairs. Nu-
merical diagnostics illustrate the distinct errors.
Conditional diffusion identities specify a restora-
tion interface, while image controls ﬁnd no iter-
ative advantage over cheap probabilistic predic-
tors. Uniform terminal rates for general second-
moment couplings remain open.
1. Introduction
Brownian stochastic interpolants construct sampling dy-
namics from paired endpoint observations. Their marginal
drift is a conditional mean, but its estimation and discretiza-
tion need not require moments of either endpoint. If X0 =
H and X1 = H+u, displacement is bounded even when H

has no ﬁrst moment. This distinction matters for singular
sources, dependent endpoint pairs, and heavy-tailed corrup-
tions in restoration (Liu et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024).
We ask which displacement moments guarantee ﬁnite-step
accuracy, and when a path comparison obscures the accu-
racy of the ﬁnal samples. For arbitrary Borel couplings,
standard Euler–Maruyama (EM) initialized at the true
source has an explicit stopped-time O(N −1) KL bound un-
der a fourth displacement moment. Conditional residuals
yield slower bounds for 2 < p < 4 and ﬁxed-law conver-
gence under a second moment, without endpoint densities
or score regularity. Learning error is measured under the
true intermediate laws.
For one ﬁxed coupling with no displacement moment
above two, the original bridge-path KL remains of order
one, its Markov projection has KL of order 1/ log N, and
terminal KL is at most C/N 2 on the same geometric grid.
These quantities observe different objects.
Sampling re-
quires the terminal marginal; the original bridge also car-
ries the source and its pairing with the target.
Markov
projection keeps every grid marginal but forgets what the
source reveals beyond the current state. Euler then replaces
each projected kernel by a Gaussian. An entropy identity
separates the resulting two path losses; signed cancellations
give the much smaller terminal bound.
This decomposition also identiﬁes a tractable second-
moment subclass. Retaining the source gives a joint-law
O((M2+d)/N) bound by an established Gaussian-channel
argument. For Markov bridge mixtures, current-state EM
inherits this bound without a source-defect term. Equal-
ity of the two residual drifts at one interior time charac-
terizes that structure, including singular endpoints. Finite-
entropy Schrödinger bridges for the probability Brownian
reference started from the prescribed source belong to this
subclass. The general second-moment rate question there-
fore remains for non-Markov couplings, where a source-
prediction gap separates the two error contracts.
Clipped partition regression from iid endpoint pairs attains
expected terminal KL consistency with fresh true-source
initialization. A lower bound rules out a uniform training-
sample rate without source structure.

1.1. Related work
Transport and regression representations for dependent in-
terpolants (Albergo et al., 2025; 2024) and source augmen-
tation (De Bortoli et al., 2023) are established. We analyze
their ﬁnite-step sampling error. Kang et al. (2026, Theo-
rems 4.1–4.4) analyze ReLU drift learning and EM sam-
pling for bounded endpoint pairs under drift Lipschitz as-
sumptions. We claim no new drift or augmentation algo-
rithm, or priority for general regression-to-generation guar-
antees.
Liu et al. (2025) already analyze standard EM for general
couplings and broader nonlinear interpolants. Their ﬁnite
bound assumes an eighth displacement moment and starts
inside the time interval. Brownian DFM bounds use eighth
endpoint moments and score integrability, with explicit d4
terms in Gentiloni Silveri et al. (2024) and d3 terms in Gen-
tiloni Silveri et al. (2026). Their full-horizon conclusions
require stronger conditions and are not implied by our early-
stopped theorem.
Weak-moment sampling is not new to Gaussian diffu-
sion models.
Chen et al. (2023, Theorem 2.2) prove
a second-moment early-stopping bound with a d2 dis-
cretization term; Benton et al. (2024, Theorem 1) obtain
nearly linear dimension dependence.
Those results con-
cern single-signal Gaussian diffusions. Equal intermedi-
ate marginals alone do not identify a two-endpoint Brow-
nian drift, diffusion coefﬁcient, or its EM discretization
(Appendix R). For a point source, however, Koike (2026,
Theorem 3.1) directly prove a second-moment bound for
standard Brownian-time EM, with a positive-time Gaussian
initialization. This overlaps the source-conditioned branch
after disintegration, subject to its initialization and error
contract; it does not directly cover our arbitrarily coupled
current-state drift.
Second-order marginal KL can already improve on path-
based bounds under smoothness assumptions (Mou et al.,
2022; Li et al., 2026).
Our separation concerns a ﬁxed
strict-M2 coupling: the projected path decays only loga-
rithmically, and the original non-Markov bridge path does
not converge in KL to EM. The marginal projection and
signed increment cancellation used in the proof are estab-
lished techniques.
The comparison-process and I–MMSE architecture is a di-
rect antecedent (Reeves & Pﬁster, 2025), building on Gaus-
sian information–estimation identities (Guo et al., 2005; Pa-
yaró & Palomar, 2009); innovations sampling also admits
certiﬁed channel-time schedules (Wainwright, 2026). For
arbitrary couplings we control conditional residuals with-
out a single-signal Markov channel; the source-conditioned
corollary identiﬁes when that reduction applies.
In the
2026 DFM result, the conditional-source assumption con-
cerns the density and score of X0 | X1; the drift still ob-
serves only (t, x). Corollary 5.1 instead retains the realized
source x0. Liu et al.’s initialization term can be zero when
the exact interior starting law is available.
Appendix R
gives the theorem-speciﬁc conditions and additional diffu-
sion comparisons.

9. Discussion
Displacement moments control sampling without inte-
grable endpoints, and source information explains why
path KL can obscure terminal accuracy. The Markov sub-
class admits a current-state O((M2 + d)/N) bound; uni-
form terminal rates and linear dimension dependence for
general couplings remain open. Learning consistency sup-
plies neither uniform sample complexity nor a guarantee
for arbitrary neural models.
These distinctions matter when choosing a bridge model.
If the goal is the terminal marginal, forgetting source infor-
mation can be compatible with accurate sampling. If the
goal includes preserving a particular source–target pairing,
the marginal alone does not describe success. The shared
marginals in Figure 1 therefore do not make the two path
laws interchangeable. The Markov result identiﬁes a set-
ting in which a current-state drift sufﬁces; it does not justify
replacing an arbitrary prescribed coupling by a Schrödinger
coupling when that pairing is part of the task.
For restoration, the guarantee controls a stopped condi-
tional law, with a bounded-Lipschitz correction to the clean
target. The image controls show no iterative advantage and
do not isolate dependence recovery. Clean-target KL, zero-
noise limits, and practical neural recovery guarantees re-
main outside the result.

R.1. Additional comparison details
Liu et al. (2025, Assumptions 4.1–4.2, Theorem 4.3, and Proposition 5.1) analyze standard Euler–Maruyama under general

broader; singular couplings and displacement-based assumptions alone are shared features.
The Brownian DFM early-stopping guarantees (Gentiloni Silveri et al., 2026, H1, H2, H4 and Theorems 2–3) use standard

T.4. Earlier image protocols and their outcomes
The earlier reused-BSDS denoising study uses 200 test originals, two 8 × 8 patches per image, Student noise with ν ∈
{2.5, 3, 10}, N ∈{4, 8, 16, 64} and three ﬁxed ﬁts. Sources and states remain untruncated, with primary output clip(YT ).
Persistent-source ﬁts improve coordinate CRPS in these conﬁgurations, but BP does not uniformly improve on EM, and
every stochastic ensemble mean has higher MSE than its matching one-call POINT predictor. The empirical-90%-coverage
gate failed; subsequent score/rank analyses are exploratory. The reproducibility archive retains all 60 prescribed CRPS
contrasts and the full protocols. Individual 95% intervals condition on ﬁxed ﬁts and draws and resample original images;
they are not simultaneous or joint-recovery guarantees.
The separate DIV2K completion study uses all 100 public validation images, four patches each, with 64 missing pixels
and bounded displacements (Agustsson & Timofte, 2017). Its four assertions were ﬁxed after the BSDS pilot failure. At
primary N = 4, BP improves fair CRPS over EM/ZERO by 3.24380%/18.07941%, and improves pooled rank-TV/CvM
over EM by 0.0462048446/0.0026287184; all four nominal Bonferroni-adjusted bootstrap lower bounds are positive. The
ﬁxed rule requires at least 1% relative point gains for both CRPS comparisons and positive bounds in all four tests; these
are not minimum effects certiﬁed by the intervals. ZERO nevertheless has lower mean MSE at every grid and in every ﬁt;
89.54% of the observed EM–BP ﬁnite-mean MSE difference at N = 4 is the estimated Monte Carlo variance difference.
Thus the results do not establish improved exact forecast-mean risk. The archive retains all four primary tests, secondary
grids and ﬁt-level contrasts.

U. Markov bridge mixtures and the second-moment rate
