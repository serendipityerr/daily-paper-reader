GENERATIVE MODELING VIA
NONLINEAR SCHRÖDINGER BRIDGES AND BRANCHING DIFFUSIONS
MATHIAS GRAU AND PIERRE HENRY-LABORDÈRE†
Abstract. We propose a new generative modeling framework grounded in forward-backward
partial differential equations (PDEs).
These equations arise naturally in mean-field game
(MFG) theory, and are closely related to nonlinear Schrödinger systems. Our main contribution
is to reinterpret this connection within the setting of generative modeling and to develop
an efficient numerical methodology for its practical implementation. Specifically, we derive
and characterize a nonlinear extension of the classical Schrödinger bridge problem through
a coupled system of PDEs, yielding a model we call SBNL (Schrödinger Bridge NonLinear).
To compute its solution, we design a simulation algorithm based on branching diffusions and
the probabilistic representation of semilinear PDEs. The resulting method is embarrassingly
parallel, scalable to high dimensions, and applicable to a broad class of nonlinear interaction
functionals. We validate the approach through numerical experiments, including recovery of
the classical Schrödinger bridge, nonlinear extensions with convex penalties (Gross-Pitaevskii)
as well as potential interaction terms, and high-dimensional generative tasks such as image
synthesis on the MNIST and FFHQ human face dataset. Furthermore, the same framework
embeds score-based generative modeling, diffusion models, and tractable gaussian Schrödinger
bridges.
1. Introduction
The Schrödinger bridge (SB) problem [1–3] has recently attracted significant attention in
generative modeling due to its ability to learn an optimally controlled stochastic dynamics that
transports between two arbitrary endpoint distributions. By minimizing relative entropy with
respect to a reference diffusion (typically Brownian motion), the SB framework reveals deep
connections between optimal transport, stochastic control, and score-based generative modeling
[4–7].
When both terminal costs and the evolving marginal law are controlled, the problem extends
beyond classical (linear) Schrödinger bridges to mean-field control and mean-field games (MFG)
[8–11].
In this regime, interactions through the marginal distribution induce a semilinear
forward–backward structure.
Existing iterative schemes for SB, such as iterative proportional fitting (IPF) / iterative
Markovian fitting (IMF) and related diffusion-based approaches [6, 7, 12], are primarily designed
for linear entropic transport.
These methods rely on alternating optimization procedures,
which can be computationally expensive and may suffer from error accumulation. More recent
approaches based on Gaussian mixture energy models [13, 14] improve scalability in some
settings but remain limited in expressivity in high dimensions. Even recent extensions based on
controlled stochastic dynamics [15] are not well suited to law-dependent running costs, which
induce intrinsically coupled semilinear dynamics.
Although recent works in MFG and generalized Schrödinger bridge formulations clarify the
structure of the coupling in terms of forward–backward potentials [16, 17], they often do not yield
objectives that are both theoretically grounded and directly amenable to unbiased stochastic
optimization in high dimensions.
Our contributions are as follows:
(1) We introduce a variational objective for semilinear mean-field Schrödinger bridges with
law-dependent running and terminal costs, and characterize optimality through coupled

1.1. Related work.
Linear Schrödinger bridges. The Schrödinger bridge problem is classically linked to entropic
optimal transport [3, 4, 18], with efficient Sinkhorn-type and iterative fitting procedures in
the linear setting. These methods scale well when dynamics are fixed between prescribed
marginals. Recent neural approaches approximate bridges through forward-backward SDEs [6]
or diffusion-style training [7, 12]; they mainly target linear bridges and often rely on expensive al-
ternating refits (IPF/IMF). A complementary, simulation-free line parameterizes the Schrödinger
potentials with Gaussian mixture forms, yielding lightweight solvers [13, 14] at the cost of limited
expressivity. Even recent drift-and-volatility extensions (Schrödinger-Bass bridges) [15] still
assume (approximately) linear entropic transport and are not tailored to running costs that
depend on the evolving marginal law.
Control formulations and mean-field games (MFG).. Once running or terminal terms depend on
the marginal law, the problem leaves the linear SB regime and sits in mean-field control / MFG
[8, 9], with forward-backward PDE couplings studied in [4, 10, 19, 20]. Extending Sinkhorn-
type or alternating schemes to such coupled HJB-Fokker-Planck systems is challenging in high
dimension. Recent interaction/unbalanced frameworks [17, 21, 22] encode growth, interaction,
or state costs through composite weighted losses and staged training. Trajectory-level optimal-
control views, e.g. adjoint matching [23], couple learning to long-horizon simulation and can be
costly. We retain entropic regularization while centering law-dependent running costs, starting
from the non linear Sinkhorn system with jointly learned coupled Schrödinger potentials, using
branching Monte Carlo.
Branching representations for semilinear PDEs. Branching diffusion methods provide probabilistic
representations of semilinear PDEs via weighted particle systems [24–26]. We extend these ideas
to the coupled forward-backward system arising in mean-field Schrödinger bridges, where the
interaction between the two potentials introduces a time-reflected dependency. This enables a
scalable estimator for both the potentials and their gradients, which we integrate into a neural
generative framework.

5.1. Implementation details. Each of φθ, ¯φη : Rd →(0, ∞) is a positive-scalar feedforward
network. Unless stated otherwise, all use 3 to 8 fully connected layers with nonlinear activations.
Widths are 16 for d = 2, 32 for d = 50, and 64 for d = 512 for latent FFHQ experiments.
Potentials are trained jointly by stochastic gradient descent on the MMD objective 14 (Adam,
γ = 10−4, B = 64). We use automatic differentiation of log φθ for sampling (Section 4.2); see
Appendix N. We train on 104 samples in 2D and 105 samples in 50D, for 300 epochs in both
cases. Sampling uses between N = 50 and N = 200 Euler steps and M ∈{103, 104} branching
trees per score evaluation (higher M in higher dimension). All runs use NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs;
bridge training takes ∼30 s for 300 epochs on 104 points in 2D, and generation ∼3 s for 50 steps.

6. Discussion
We formulate semilinear mean-field Schrödinger bridges with law-dependent f(m(t, ·)), g(m(T, ·))
and characterize optimality through coupled (φ, ¯φ) PDEs, equivalently a Lasry - Lions (m, v)
system – beyond the linear entropic setting of Sinkhorn and iterative Markovian fitting. We
recast the terminal conditions as a nonlinear Sinkhorn system, which we solve by Maximum
Mean Discrepancy, using branching diffusions to obtain unbiased stochastic representations of φ.
Our experiments cover bridge recovery, Gross-Pitaevskii-type couplings, potential interactions,
and latent transport.
The numerics are encouraging, but we do not claim systematic superiority over mature
diffusion Schrödinger-bridge pipelines in the linear setting. Training in the nonlinear regime still
requires careful tuning of the hyperparameters (branching rate c, kernel bandwidths, penalty λ)
to remain stable, and both the parallelization of the branching simulation and the variance of
the underlying Monte Carlo estimators leave room for improvement. We see this work as a new
variational and optimality system whose first mesh-free realization invites variance reduction
and hybridization with standard training pipelines.

H.4. Lifted (semilinear) Schrödinger bridge and Gyöngy’s Markov projection. Gyöngy’s
Markov projection theorem [37] has played a central role in mathematical finance. It provides a
way to construct a Markov diffusion in Rd with prescribed marginal distributions at each time.
This idea underlies the calibration of local volatility and local stochastic volatility models, as
pioneered by Dupire’s formula [38] and systematically developed in the monograph [39].
In this subsection, we apply Gyöngy’s projection to the setting of Schrödinger bridges. The key
observation is that one may lift the marginals µ0, µT to an augmented space Rd+1 (eventually
higher), solve the Schrödinger bridge there, and then project back onto Rd. Gyöngy’s mimicking
theorem, in the form of Brunick and Shreve [40], then provides a solution of a Markovian SDE
in Rd with the correct marginals µ0, µT . Although this projected diffusion has the right one-time
marginals, it is in general not itself a Schrödinger bridge. It therefore defines a new class of
generative models, obtained by combining lifted SB with Markov projection.

Appendix M. Feynman-Galton-Watson diagrams
Each realization of the branching system is a Galton-Watson tree: lines correspond to
Brownian trajectories between branching events, and vertices represent branchings. This is
directly analogous to tree Feynman diagrams: dashed lines depict white particles (φ), solid lines
depict black particles ( ¯φ), and trivalent vertices encode the cubic nonlinearity. For the cubic
nonlinearity Fp(m) = m in (10) (i.e. a0 = 0, a1 = 1, and we take p1 = 1), the only branching is
k = 1:

these vertices yields a perturbative expansion in the coupling constant c: a diagram with n
branchings contributes a weight of order cn.
Importantly, if we truncate the Galton-Watson expansion to order 0 (no branching), the dynamics
are given only by free Brownian propagation. In this case, the system reduces to the classical
Schrödinger bridge, and the PDEs collapse to the linear forward-backward heat equations.
Higher-order branchings then represent nonlinear corrections beyond this baseline. Appendix K
gives a scalar toy sanity check of the branching scheme for the T−t coupling in (3)-(4).

Appendix N. Implementation details
Training and sampling both rely on stochastic quantities built from branching trees and on
differentiating log bφ(t, x) with respect to x (Algorithm 2). When coordinates have very different
scales or heavy tails, (i) products of terminal factors in the branching estimator can span many
orders of magnitude, inflating Monte Carlo variance, and (ii) autograd through log φθ and
the reweighted score becomes ill-conditioned relative to the isotropic unit-variance reference
noise in the SDE. We therefore normalize inputs before bridge estimation, using one of the two
mechanisms below depending on dimensionality and data modality.

Training and sampling. Because F is state- but not density-dependent, the system (3)-(4) is
linear and φ(0, x) is estimated by the reweighted trees above; the potentials are trained with the
MMD objective (14). Two practical ingredients make the importance-sampled estimator stable.
First, the interior weights W = bA ¯φη/µ∗(resp. bB φθ/µ∗) are heavy-tailed when µ∗is too narrow,
so we take a wide Gaussian reference µ∗covering the region actually explored by the branching
diffusion. Second, we use self-normalized importance sampling (dividing each weight vector by
its own sum, gradient preserved), which turns the interior expectations into low-variance ratio
estimators comparing the normalized residual measures. At sampling time, the drift ∇log φ
is estimated by the same weighted trees (Algorithm 2), so V deflects trajectories away from
high-V regions.
Endpoint offset trick. When V has an asymmetric geometry (e.g. gates at different heights, a
curved valley), we place µ0 and µT at different anchor points aligned with the entrance/exit of
the low-energy corridor, rather than symmetrically. This gives the score a clear valley to follow
through the obstacle. Transporting mass over a distance ∼ℓrequires the drift to integrate to
∼ℓ, so we also use a longer horizon T (with a small rate c): too small a T leaves the drift no
time to route the mass around the barrier.
