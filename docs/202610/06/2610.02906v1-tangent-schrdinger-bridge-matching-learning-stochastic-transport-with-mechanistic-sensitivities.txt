ABSTRACT
Predicting how stochastic systems respond to changes in viscosity, reaction rates,
or external forces requires costly simulations, motivating reusable learned mod-
els. Yet matching observed outcome distributions does not ensure accurate in-
tervention responses.
We introduce Tangent Schr¨odinger Bridge Matching
(Tangent-SBM), which learns stochastic transports from endpoint observations
and mechanistic sensitivities. It propagates parameter derivatives alongside tra-
jectories and supervises them against supplied targets. For average-response tar-
gets, single-rollout squared error also penalizes response variability; our objective
uses two independent rollouts to match the mean without this additional penalty.
We establish conditions under which sensitivity accuracy bounds finite-change
prediction error and decision regret. Across Gaussian, stochastic double-well,
PDEBench reaction–diffusion, and stochastic Navier–Stokes systems, Tangent-
SBM improves sensitivity and finite-change prediction over matched conditional-
bridge baselines while maintaining comparable endpoint and distributional accu-
racy. Controls examine target correctness, response objectives, and simulator-
budget allocation. To test decision usefulness, we evaluate calibrated viscosity
selection in Navier–Stokes: Tangent-SBM reduces tracking error relative to tak-
ing no action on every evaluated task.

INTRODUCTION
Scientific simulators use mathematical models to predict how a system evolves; for example, how
a fluid flows at a given viscosity. An intervention changes a parameter or operating condition. In
stochastic systems, random forcing or fluctuations produce different outcomes even with the same
inputs. Comparing interventions can therefore require many numerical solves for each candidate
and initial condition, making repeated optimization or decision-making costly. A reusable surro-
gate shifts computation to training and can then evaluate new candidates more cheaply (O’Leary-
Roseberry et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025). For stochastic systems, such a surrogate should represent
the distribution of possible outcomes, not only their mean.
Accurate predictions at observed parameter values do not, however, ensure accurate responses to
parameter changes. For example, a surrogate used to choose an emission rate must predict how
adjusting that rate changes pollutant concentrations. A model can fit the available observations
while recommending an ineffective intervention.
For small parameter changes, this response is described by a derivative, or sensitivity. In a stochas-
tic system, it may describe an individual trajectory’s response with random inputs held fixed, or
the average response across trajectories. Physical models and simulators supply such derivatives
through analytic expressions, differentiation, or numerical perturbations. We call this information
mechanistic sensitivities.
Schr¨odinger bridges learn stochastic processes connecting prescribed endpoint distributions while
remaining close to a reference process (L´eonard, 2014). Neural and conditional formulations make
them useful for learning parameter-dependent outcome distributions (De Bortoli et al., 2021; Shi
et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2022). Yet endpoint agreement at finitely many parameter

How should mechanistic sensitivities constrain a learned stochastic transport?
We introduce Tangent Schr¨odinger Bridge Matching (Tangent-SBM), which propagates param-
eter sensitivities alongside learned trajectories and supervises their terminal responses. These re-
sponses can vary across trajectories: for example, changing emissions may affect pollutant concen-
trations differently under different wind conditions. If supervision specifies only the average effect,
forcing every trajectory to match it also suppresses response variability. Our two-rollout objective
instead matches the average without this additional penalty.
Because sensitivities and endpoint observations come from the same simulator, we compare re-
sponse labels with spending that budget on more outcome samples. We also test direct predictors
when only a mean, such as average pollutant concentration, is needed; Tangent-SBM additionally
models the distribution of possible outcomes.
Our contributions are:
• Response-supervised stochastic transport. We introduce Tangent-SBM with distinct ob-
jectives for individual and average response targets.
• From sensitivities to interventions. We show why matching observed outcomes can leave
parameter responses incorrect, and establish when accurate sensitivities lead to accurate
predictions of intervention effects and better decisions.
• Controlled evaluation across four systems. Gaussian, double-well, reaction–diffusion,
and stochastic Navier–Stokes experiments assess response accuracy, endpoint fidelity, and,
for stochastic systems, distributional fit. Controls test target correctness and simulation-
budget allocation; calibrated intervention selection tests downstream usefulness.

RELATED WORK
Schr¨odinger bridges and stochastic generative models.
Schr¨odinger bridges connect endpoint
distributions through a process close to a reference diffusion (L´eonard, 2014), linking stochastic
transport with entropy-regularized optimal transport (Cuturi, 2013; Peyr´e & Cuturi, 2019). Neu-
ral solvers include deep generative bridges, diffusion-based iterative fitting, and DSBM (Wang
et al., 2021; De Bortoli et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2023), with extensions to conditional sampling and
simulation-free training (Shi et al., 2022; Tong et al., 2024). Generalized and twisted formulations
incorporate trajectory costs or potentials (Liu et al., 2022; 2024; Noble et al., 2026). Related dif-
fusion, flow-matching, stochastic-interpolant, and neural-SDE models provide alternative ways to
learn distributions and stochastic dynamics (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021; Lipman et al., 2023;

Albergo et al., 2023; Kidger et al., 2021). Tangent-SBM adds mechanistic sensitivity supervision to
constrain intervention responses beyond endpoint fitting.
Scientific surrogates and derivative-informed learning.
Physics-informed learning incorporates
governing equations into training (Raissi et al., 2019; Karniadakis et al., 2021), while standard de-
terministic neural operators map input functions to output fields (Lu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021;
Kovachki et al., 2023). Conditional stochastic bridges instead model a distribution of possible out-
comes for the same input. Physical constraints also inform diffusion-based field reconstruction (Li
et al., 2025). Explicit derivative supervision is central to Sobolev training and Jacobian matching
(Czarnecki et al., 2017; Srinivas & Fleuret, 2018), and derivative-informed neural operators apply
this principle to scientific surrogates (O’Leary-Roseberry et al., 2024). Subsequent work demon-
strates its use in optimization under uncertainty and develops derivative-informed Fourier neural
operators (Luo et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2026). Tangent-SBM distinguishes individual and mean re-
sponses in stochastic transports. Deterministic controls test response supervision beyond bridges;
stochastic experiments assess both outcome distributions and intervention responses.
Stochastic sensitivities and response objectives.
Automatic differentiation and SDE sensitivity
methods provide tools for differentiating numerical stochastic models (Baydin et al., 2018; Li et al.,
2020). The training objective must additionally reflect the supplied target: regressing each random
response against a mean target with squared error penalizes response variance alongside mean error.
We identify this effect and use independent rollouts to isolate the mean-error term. The construction
follows the established double-sampling principle for squared expected residuals (Baird, 1995); our
contribution is its formulation and evaluation within mechanistic response supervision for stochastic
bridges.

Response supervision uses free rollouts: the learned model generates its terminal state without a
prescribed endpoint. Unlike trajectories constrained to reach sampled endpoints, these measure the
response of the sampler used for prediction. Appendix E compares the two constructions.
Each direction adds a tangent array with the same dimensions as the state and additional derivative
computations; mean supervision also requires independent replicas. Matching simulator-call bud-
gets therefore does not imply matching training cost. The added loss balances endpoint and response
fit and does not generally preserve the original DSBM solution.

FROM LOCAL SENSITIVITIES TO FINITE INTERVENTIONS
We show why endpoint agreement leaves intervention responses undetermined and establish when
sensitivity accuracy controls finite-change prediction error and decision regret. Fix the context c
and write mθ(u) and m∗(u) for the model and physical mean outcomes, with Jacobians Jθ and J∗.
Proofs appear in Appendix A.
What endpoint observations leave undetermined.
Proposition 1 (Endpoint ambiguity). For finitely many distinct interventions u1, . . . , uk ∈Rp, there
exist smooth families of conditional distributions with finite means that agree at every ui but have
arbitrarily different mean Jacobians at any chosen ui.
Thus, endpoint agreement alone does not identify intervention responses without further assump-
tions. Sensitivity supervision supplies information about this otherwise unconstrained parameter
dependence.

EXPERIMENTS
We test whether mechanistic supervision improves intervention responses beyond endpoint fitting,
which aspects of supervision matter, and whether the learned responses support useful decisions.
5.1
SYSTEMS AND EVALUATION
Each benchmark predicts an outcome from an initial state and an intervention. Endpoint examples
supervise outcomes; sensitivity targets supervise how those outcomes change with the intervention.
We use two low-dimensional systems and two spatial-field systems.
Conditional Gaussian.
We construct a two-dimensional Gaussian outcome:
Y ∗(x0, u) = Ax0 + B1u + B2u2 + B3 sin(πu) + ϵ,
ϵ ∼N(0, Σ).
(16)
Here x0 is the initial state, u is the scalar intervention, A, B1, B2, B3 are fixed coefficients, and ϵ is
Gaussian noise with covariance Σ. Endpoint training uses u ∈{−1, 0, 1}, where the sinusoidal term
vanishes but its derivative does not. The analytic sensitivity therefore reveals parameter dependence
absent from the training outcomes, providing a controlled example of Proposition 1.
Stochastic double well.
We use a double-well Langevin system (Pavliotis, 2014):

Here Xτ is the particle position, Bτ is standard Brownian motion, and τ denotes physical time,
distinct from bridge time. The intervention u tilts the potential, favoring one of its two wells. We
predict the terminal distribution from the initial position and intervention. Targets average simulator
trajectory derivatives to describe the response of the mean outcome.

Evaluation conditions.
Seen uses endpoint-training intervention values with fresh evaluation ex-
amples; in-distribution (ID) uses additional values inside that range. Near-OOD and Far-OOD use
values progressively farther outside it. These labels describe intervention values. Response supervi-
sion may extend beyond the endpoint-training range; both domains are specified in Appendix B.

Baselines.
We compare with unconditional DSBM, Conditional DSBM, GSBM, and TSBM (Shi
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Noble et al., 2026). These four baselines receive no sensitivity targets;
all except unconditional DSBM receive intervention inputs. GSBM and TSBM use trajectory costs

response targets as Tangent-SBM but minimizes each rollout’s squared response error. We evaluate
this control on double well and SNS, where Tangent-SBM uses two-rollout mean supervision. On
Gaussian and PDEBench, Tangent-SBM already uses single-rollout squared response matching.

ENDPOINT FIT AND INTERVENTION RESPONSE
On Gaussian ID, Tangent-SBM reduces EJ from 0.866 to 0.050 relative to Conditional DSBM,
while endpoint RMSE decreases from 0.433 to 0.236. GSBM and TSBM have response errors close
to the conditional baseline. The analytic construction illustrates why fitting the observed endpoint
distributions need not recover the missing parameter dependence.
On the double well, Tangent-SBM reduces ID response error from 0.304 to 0.197, with endpoint
RMSE decreasing from 0.148 to 0.136. GSBM and TSBM attain response errors of 0.281 and
0.288. The improvement over Conditional DSBM is larger on Near-OOD (1.205 versus 0.303),
while Tangent-SBM’s Far-OOD error remains 0.984. On PDEBench, response error decreases by
37–44% across evaluated splits relative to Conditional DSBM, with comparable or lower endpoint
errors. The selected GSBM and TSBM adaptations also have larger response errors in these runs.

5.3
WHAT MAKES RESPONSE SUPERVISION EFFECTIVE?
Matching the meaning of response targets.
Sobolev-DSBM (single-rollout) matches each tra-
jectory response to the supplied mean target. Tangent-SBM instead matches the average response
without the additional variance penalty. On double well, this reduces ID mean-response RMSE from
0.104 ± 0.025 to 0.066 ± 0.014. Appendix E reports response variability and compares supervision
through free and endpoint-constrained rollouts.
Correct targets and response coverage.
On PDEBench ID, assigning sensitivity targets to the
wrong examples or reversing their signs increases EJ from 0.508 to 1.010 and 1.647, respectively.
Thus, adding a derivative loss with incorrect information does not reproduce the gains.
On SNS, retaining 25%, 50%, or all additional response-supervision locations gives ID errors of
2.075, 1.726, and 1.002. All variants retain response labels at the endpoint-training intervention val-
ues. The decreasing mean errors support broader response coverage in this experiment. Appendix F
reports seed variability, results across systems, and SNS direction and magnitude diagnostics, dis-
tinguishing better alignment from merely smaller predictions.

do not recover the gains from explicit response supervision. Appendix G specifies the matched
simulator budgets and training procedures.
Response supervision beyond bridges.
Adding response supervision reduces ID sensitivity error
from 0.865 to 0.017 for the Gaussian terminal predictor and from 0.416 to 0.204 for the PDEBench
terminal predictor. Direct response-supervised predictors are strong alternatives when only mean
outcomes are needed; Tangent-SBM additionally retains a stochastic model of outcome distribu-
tions. Appendix H compares terminal predictors, deterministic operators, and conditional flow
matching across systems. Appendix I examines forward and backward response supervision in the
Gaussian system.
5.4
INTERVENTION SELECTION
We select a log-viscosity change from {0, ±0.125, ±0.25} to approach a desired SNS mean-field
change. Each method selects the action whose calibrated local-response prediction is closest to
the goal. The simulator evaluates the chosen action; the oracle selects the best candidate using
simulator-estimated outcomes.
We compare Tangent-SBM with Conditional DSBM, TSBM (the strongest SNS endpoint baseline),
and unconditional DSBM, whose zero response selects no intervention. Testing uses 32 tasks from
16 contexts with two goals each, shared across three training seeds. Appendix B details calibration
and evaluation.
Table 2 shows that Tangent-SBM reduces tracking error, the distance from the desired change, by
54% relative to Conditional DSBM and 65% relative to TSBM. It improves over no intervention on
all 96 seed–task pairs and selects the simulator’s best candidate action in 79.2% of decisions. Its
normalized regret, measuring additional squared tracking loss relative to that oracle, is 0.048. These
results demonstrate useful intervention selection.
6
DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
Tangent-SBM shows how mechanistic sensitivities can complement endpoint observations to im-
prove intervention responses in stochastic transport. Across four systems, the comparisons with
conditional bridges and simulator-budget controls support the value of explicit response supervision.
On the stochastic benchmarks, these gains coexist with distributional fit comparable to Conditional
DSBM. The SNS decision study further shows that learned responses can guide useful intervention
choices.

responds when predicting an endpoint on its own.

How much response coverage is needed?
Response labels can be supplied at additional inter-
vention settings beyond those used for endpoint training. We retain 25%, 50%, or 100% of this
additional response-label pool to examine how coverage affects accuracy. For SNS, labels at the
endpoint-training settings are retained in every variant; only the additional conditions are subsam-
pled.
Table 9 reports the resulting sensitivity errors. Here 100% means the full available label pool, not
every possible intervention.
Gaussian, double well, and PDEBench show little change in response error across the tested cover-
age levels in Table 9, suggesting that 25% of the additional supervision locations suffice to maintain
similar accuracy in these settings. SNS benefits more from broader coverage; its mean sensitivity
error decreases as more response conditions are included.

response. Thus, the benefit of correct targets is not explained solely by predicting smaller changes;
their direction also matters.

supervision settings, while SpreadBudget distributes the same simulation budget over a broader
intervention range. Neither control uses sensitivity labels. Table 11 reports the resulting prediction
and response errors.
PDEBench allocation.
The response-label set contains 108 anchor conditions, at intervention set-

simulator runs at slightly perturbed intervention values. The additional budget is therefore

ples, rather than using their differences as sensitivity targets. SpreadBudget instead uses 648 runs

[−1.3, 1.3]3. Each control therefore has 918 endpoint examples.

RESPONSE SUPERVISION BEYOND BRIDGES
We examine whether response supervision benefits predictors beyond stochastic bridges. Direct
terminal predictors map an initial condition and intervention to an outcome without integrating a
learned bridge. Deterministic field operators similarly predict an output field directly. For each
deterministic predictor, we compare endpoint-only training with training that additionally uses sen-
sitivity targets.
We also evaluate conditional flow matching, a stochastic generative model conditioned on the inter-
vention, without response supervision. This provides an additional model comparison, rather than a
paired test of response supervision. Table 12 reports the results.
Comparison setup.
The PDEBench terminal variants continue from the same initial checkpoint.
The separate PDEBench operator comparison uses 1,600 training updates per variant. Deterministic
predictions require no averaging over model noise; stochastic mean predictions use the sampling
budgets specified in Appendix B.
Benefits across predictor families.
Table 12 shows lower mean endpoint, sensitivity, and finite-
change errors with response supervision in every tested deterministic predictor pair. For example,
sensitivity error decreases from 0.865 to 0.017 for the Gaussian terminal predictor and from 0.416 to
0.204 for the PDEBench terminal predictor. The SNS operator also improves substantially, although
its response errors remain above the zero-response reference. These comparisons show that the
benefits of mechanistic response information extend beyond bridge learning.

ADDITIONAL SUPERVISION STUDIES
Forward and backward response supervision.
The learned bridge includes a forward transport
from initial to terminal states and a backward transport in the reverse direction. A model that re-
sponds accurately to interventions in one direction need not do so in the other.
For the Gaussian system, the reference conditional distribution in the reverse direction is available
analytically, so we can also compute its intervention sensitivity. Table 13 compares training with
forward-response targets, backward-response targets, or both. These directions refer to the learned
transport, not to reversing the physical simulator’s time.
Table 13 shows that supervising one direction mainly improves the response in that direction.
Forward-only supervision gives low forward error but leaves a large backward error; backward-
only supervision shows the opposite pattern. Supervising both directions gives low response errors
in both and the lowest mean endpoint RMSE in this study. Forward-only training retains the lowest
mean forward response error.
These results show that response supervision can be applied to either transport direction, and that
accuracy in the unsupervised direction does not follow automatically.
