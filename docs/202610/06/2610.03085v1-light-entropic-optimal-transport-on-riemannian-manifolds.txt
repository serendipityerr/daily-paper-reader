## ABSTRACT 

Entropic Optimal Transport (EOT) has become a practical framework for learning stochastic couplings between complex distributions, with applications in genera-tive modeling and domain adaptation. However, most EOT solvers are designed for Euclidean spaces, while manifold extensions remain limited and often rely on costly iterative methods, simulated dynamics, or generic neural models that do not fully exploit the underlying geometry. We introduce ManifoldLightOT, a light approach for learning kernel-induced EOT couplings directly on common mani-folds. Using the kernel form of the EOT solution, we construct geometry-specific Gibbs kernels together with compatible potential parameterizations for spheres, tori, SO(3) , and SE(3) . These choices yield closed-form normalization and di-rectly sampleable conditional distributions. Our formulation naturally extends to products of manifolds, making it applicable to more complex geometries. The pa-rameters of the potentials are optimized directly from samples using Monte Carlo estimates of the learning objective. Through synthetic and real-world experiments, we show that ManifoldLightOT often outperforms existing manifold OT methods while retaining direct sampling. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

In recent years, developing computationally efficient solvers for Optimal Transport (OT) and En-tropic Optimal Transport (EOT) problems (Cuturi, 2013; Villani, 2008) has attracted considerable interest. OT seeks a coupling between prescribed source and target distributions that minimizes an expected transportation cost, while EOT augments this objective with an entropic regularization term. A well-established class of solvers operates on discrete probability distributions, representing the coupling as a matrix whose dimensions depend on the numbers of source and target samples; see Peyr´ e et al. (2019) for a survey. In this work, we focus on continuous solvers (Gushchin et al., 2023b; Korotin et al., 2021), which learn conditional distributions or transport maps (plans), typi-cally parameterized by neural networks, from samples of the source and target distributions. The EOT formulation is equivalent to the static Schr¨ odinger bridge (SB) problem (Schr¨ odinger, 1931; 1932; L´ eonard, 2013) when the transport cost is induced by the reference measure of the SB. Several neural approaches have been proposed in recent years to solve the EOT or SB problems (Mokrov et al., 2024; Bortoli et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024; Gushchin et al., 2023a; Korotin et al., 2024; Persiianov et al., 2026). These solvers support out-of-sample estimation, enabling applica-tions such as image generation, image-to-image translation, domain adaptation, and biological tasks including modeling cell dynamics and predicting cellular responses to perturbations. However, these methods are formulated in Euclidean spaces, where source and target samples are elements of RD .However, these methods may not be directly adjusted when data are supported on curved manifolds. Switching on manifold support has proved effective across several generative modeling frameworks, including flow matching (Chen & Lipman, 2024), continuous normalizing flows (Mathieu & Nickel, 2020), and score-based diffusion models (Bortoli et al., 2022). Manifold-aware formulations have also been developed for neural OT and EOT, notably Riemannian Neural OT (RNOT) (Micheli et al., 2026a) and Entropic Riemannian Neural OT (ERNOT) (Micheli et al., 2026b), respectively. In prac-tice, RNOT approximates the c-transform through an additional inner optimization, while ERNOT relies on minibatch or finite-support approximations of the soft c-transform and the induced Gibbs conditionals. These approximations can introduce additional optimization error, sampling bias, and 1arXiv:2610.03085v1 [cs.LG] 2 Oct 2026 Preprint sensitivity to the chosen target support in high-dimensional settings. Motivated by these observa-tions, we propose ManifoldLightOT, an EOT solver with geometry-specific potential parameteriza-tions for several classes of Riemannian manifolds. Following the terminology of LightSB (Korotin et al., 2024), we use the word “light” to denote parameterizations that yield closed-form expressions for the normalizing term and conditional distribution of the EOT coupling. Our main contribution is ManifoldLightOT, a light solver for kernel-induced EOT problems on spheres, tori, SO(3) , and SE(3) . The method combines geometry-specific Gibbs kernels with com-patible potential families, yielding closed-form normalization functions and directly sampleable conditional distributions. We further show that these tractability properties are preserved under Cartesian products, extending the construction to heterogeneous product manifolds. The resulting solver is trained using Monte Carlo estimates of the learning objective from unpaired source and target samples, without additional inner optimization problems. We evaluate ManifoldLightOT on synthetic transport problems across the considered manifolds and on real-world manifold-valued data, comparing its performance with other existing OT methods.

## 3 MANIFOLD ENTROPIC OPTIMAL TRANSPORT SOLVER 

The design of ManifoldLightOT is motivated by the observation that tractability (parameterized closed-form solution, direct sampling) in continuous EOT depends on the compatibility between the Gibbs kernel and its associated probability family. In the Euclidean setting, LightSB (Korotin et al., 2024) exploits this compatibility for the quadratic cost through Gaussian kernels and Gaussian 3Preprint mixtures. We adopt this insight as a design criterion for manifold-valued transport. Our method specifies the EOT problem through geometry-aware Gibbs kernels. Each kernel Kε induces the corresponding cost cε(x, y ) = −ε log Kε(x, y ), up to additive constants. The goal is therefore not to approximate squared-geodesic EOT on every manifold, but to construct tractable kernel-induced EOT problems whose associated probability families admit closed-form normalization and conditional sampling. We apply this principle for spheres, tori, SO(3) , SE(3) , and their Cartesian products. We derive the learning objective in §3.1, introduce the manifold-specific constructions in §3.2, and describe training and sampling in §3.3. 3.1 LEARNING OBJECTIVE

## 4 RELATED WORKS 

In this section, we review two categories of continuous optimal transport methods most relevant to our setting. We first discuss general continuous OT and EOT solvers in §4.1, which learn trans-port maps or coupling representations from samples and support out-of-sample inference. We then consider Riemannian-aware OT solvers in §4.2, which explicitly incorporate the geometry of the underlying manifold into the formulation. 4.1 CONTINOUS OT S OLVERS 

Continuous neural OT methods provide a flexible framework for learning transport directly from samples. In Seguy et al. (2018) develop a stochastic optimization approach for large-scale regular-ized OT, while Daniels et al. (2025) use score-based models to sample from regularized transport couplings. Neural Optimal Transport (NeuralOT) (Korotin et al., 2023) introduces a minimax for-mulation for learning transport maps and plans under strong and weak transport costs. This frame-work is further generalized by NeuralOT with General Cost Functionals (Asadulaev et al., 2024), which accommodates a broader class of transport objectives beyond standard pointwise costs. For entropy-regularized OT, Energy-guided Entropic NOT (Mokrov et al., 2024) parameterizes condi-tional transport plans using energy-based models and supports general continuous ground costs. For comparison, we consider NeuralOT as a representative general continuous OT baseline. 4.2 CONTINOUS RIEMMANIAN OT S OLVERS 

Riemannian Neural Optimal Transport (RNOT) (Micheli et al., 2026a) learns continuous optimal transport maps directly on Riemannian manifolds for the squared geodesic cost. It parameterizes a continuous potential whose c-transform enforces c-concavity and constructs the transport map 

T (x), allowing the learned map to transport previously unseen source samples without discretizing the manifold. Entropic Riemannian Neural Optimal Transport (ERNOT) (Micheli et al., 2026b) solves entropically regularized OT on Riemannian manifolds by learning a neural Schr¨ odinger po-tential that defines the transport coupling. For each source point, this coupling gives a conditional distribution over target points. When a single transported point is required, ERNOT summarizes this distribution using manifold-aware constructions, such as its Riemannian barycenter or the mode of a heat-smoothed version of the conditional distribution.

## 6 DISCUSSION 

ManifoldLightOT combines strong empirical performance with a simpler training and sampling procedure than existing manifold-aware EOT solvers. The experiments show that the proposed kernel-induced EOT formulations adapt well across the considered geometries, including spheres, tori, SO(3) , and SE(3) . By using manifold-specific kernel-potential pairs, the method avoids inner optimization procedures and can be trained from Monte Carlo estimates of the learning objective. It is important to note that the proposed pipeline, learning objective, and sampling procedure are not tied to a particular manifold. Extending ManifoldLightOT to a new geometry therefore amounts to deriving an appropriate kernel-potential pair that preserves tractable normalization and direct conditional sampling. This derivation is the main manifold-specific challenge of our formulation and can require additional analytical work. At the same time, the induced costs are not specific to ManifoldLightOT: they define valid geometry-aware EOT objectives and could, in principle, be incorporated into other continuous OT/EOT solvers that support general costs. Synthetic SE(3) . A point in SE(3) consists of a three-dimensional rotation and a three-dimensional translation, and is represented in our implementation by a unit quaternion q ∈ S3 together with a translation vector t ∈ R3. Continental Drift. We follow the continental-drift benchmark of Cohen et al. (2021) using paleo-geographic reconstructions from GPlates (M¨ uller et al., 2018). The source distribution corresponds to continental regions at 150 million years ago, while the target corresponds to present-day conti-nental regions. The reconstructions are provided as GeoJSON files containing polygonal continental boundaries in geographic coordinates, with each polygon represented by a sequence of longitude-latitude vertices. Thus, the raw data do not consist of points on S2; instead, these polygons define the continental regions from which spherical samples are obtained. Points are sampled uniformly with respect to surface area on S2 and restricted to the corresponding continental regions by rejection sampling. Specifically, we draw a longitude λ ∼ Unif[ −π, π ) and a scalar z ∼ Unif[ −1, 1] . The latitude is then φ = arcsin z, and the corresponding point on the unit sphere is 

x = (cos φ cos λ, cos φ sin λ, sin φ).

This construction is uniform with respect to spherical surface area. The longitude-latitude coordi-nates of each candidate point are tested against the continental polygons for the corresponding time period, and candidates outside all polygons are rejected. Accepted points are represented by their three-dimensional unit vectors x ∈ S2 for training and evaluation. The procedure is applied inde-pendently to the 150 Ma and present-day polygons, yielding unpaired empirical source and target distributions. We sample 50 ,000 training points from each distribution. A.2.3 CRYSTALLOGRAPHIC TEXTURE DATASET 

We use the Ti-6Al-4V electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) dataset of Zeng et al. (2025), which contains crystallographic orientation maps for the starting material and for samples subjected to dif-ferent hot-rolling conditions. The raw measurements are provided as EBSD maps in the DefDAP format, with each spatial measurement associated with a crystallographic orientation and a phase label. In our experiments, we use the starting material as the source state and the material rolled at 

825 ◦C with a 75% thickness reduction as the target state. We construct two transport tasks corre-sponding to the rolling-direction (RD) and transverse-direction (TD) sections: for each section, the source distribution is formed by the crystallographic orientations measured in the starting material, while the target distribution is formed by the orientations measured after the 825 ◦C, 75% -reduction treatment. These data are naturally unpaired because the source and target orientations are obtained from separate EBSD maps acquired at different material states; consequently, an orientation mea-sured at a given spatial location in the starting material has no one-to-one correspondence with an orientation in the rolled sample. The learning problem is therefore to transport between the two empirical orientation distributions rather than between matched orientation pairs. We preprocess each EBSD map using DefDAP . The texture index, also known as the J-index, is widely used to quantify the strength or concentration of an orientation distribution, while the M-index was introduced by Skemer et al. A.4 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

For each experiment, all methods use the same training and test distributions and are evaluated on the same test samples. Unless stated otherwise, we use minibatches of size 256 . ManifoldLightOT, ERNOT, and NeuralOT are implemented in PyTorch, while RNOT follows its original JAX/Flax implementation. All experiments are run on a single GPU. The hyperparameters described below are those used for the reported experiments. 

ManifoldLightOT. ManifoldLightOT directly optimizes the parameters of the geometry-specific mixture potentials introduced in §3.2, without using an additional neural network. The mixture components are initialized from randomly selected target training samples, with initially uniform mixture weights, and all parameters are optimized with Adam using β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .999 , and no weight decay. 

Synthetic experiments. For the high-dimensional sphere and torus experiments, we use K = 30 

components in dimension 2 and K = 10 D components for D ∈ { 10 , 20 , 40 , 60 , 80 , 100 }, with batch size 256 . We train for 50000 steps for D ≤ 20 , 100000 steps for D ∈ { 40 , 60 , 80 }, and 200000 

steps for D = 100 . The learning rate is 10 −3 for the low- and medium-dimensional configurations and 10 −2 for the higher-dimensional configurations. We use ε = 0 .1 up to dimension 40 and 

ε = 0 .01 for dimensions 60 –100 , with T2 also using ε = 0 .01 . For the synthetic SO(3) and SE(3) 

experiments, we use K = 100 components, batch size 256 , and train for 50 ,000 steps with learning rate 10 −2, using ε = 0 .05 and 0.2, respectively. The SO(3) rotational kernel scale is λR = 1 , while for SE(3) we use rotational scale 1 and translational scale 0.16 .

Continental Drift. We use K = 1000 components, ε = 0 .1, batch size 64 , and train for 10 6 steps with learning rate 10 −2.

Crystallographic texture. We use K = 1000 components, ε = 0 .01 , rotational kernel scale λR = 2 ,batch size 256 , and train for 200000 steps with learning rate 10 −2.20 Preprint

NeuralOT. Our NeuralOT implementation is based on the official implementation ( https:// github.com/iamalexkorotin/NeuralOptimalTransport ), adapted to the manifold representations and transport costs considered in this work. Both the transport map T and the po-tential f are represented by MLPs with three hidden layers and ReLU activations. The transport network maps between the corresponding manifold coordinate representations and its output is pro-jected back to the manifold, while the potential network outputs a scalar.
