# A Self-Coupling Gaussian Extremal Inequality for Information-Constrained Optimal Transport with Applications to Rate-Distortion-Perception 

Jiayang Zou, Jun Chen, Senior Member, IEEE, Ayfer ¨ Ozg¨ ur, Senior Member, IEEE, Jia Wang 

Abstract 

How much mutual information is needed to achieve a target correlation between two copies of a prescribed distribution? We prove a sharp Gaussian extremal inequality for this self-coupling problem: among distributions with a fixed mean and covariance, the Gaussian has the greatest minimum information cost for meeting a positive semidefinite cross-covariance target. The inequality is strict for non-Gaussian distributions when the target is positive definite. The proof uses a midpoint gluing construction. Two Ornstein–Uhlenbeck half-bridges, each started from the prescribed endpoint law, meet at a common intermediate distribution and are joined by conditional gluing. Changing the Gaussian reference then turns the resulting relative-entropy bound into the desired extremal inequality. As a first application, scaling one endpoint of the self-coupling establishes Gaussian extremality for rate-distortion-perception with unlimited common randomness, under quadratic distortion and squared Wasserstein-2 perception measures. We also establish an analogous result for finite common randomness, where the problem involves two mutual information constraints associated with an auxiliary random variable. In that setting, we retain an intermediate observation on one half-bridge and balance the two information budgets simultaneously using the Poincar´ e-Miranda theorem. 

Index Terms 

Gaussian extremality, information-constrained optimal transport, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, rate-distortion-perception theory, self-coupling. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Information-constrained optimal transport asks how much two prescribed distributions can be brought into agreement using a limited amount of statistical dependence. Its basic problem, as defined in [1], is 

inf   

> π∈Π( μ,ν )
> Iπ(X;Y)≤R

Eπ c(X, Y ), (1) where Π( μ, ν ) is the set of couplings of μ and ν, c is a transport cost, and Iπ (X; Y ) = KL( π ∥ μ ⊗ ν) is the mutual information under π, with KL denoting Kullback–Leibler divergence (also known as relative entropy). Without the mutual information constraint, (1) reduces to the classical Kantorovich transport problem. The role of the information constraint is especially clear in the self-coupling case ν = μ. Under quadratic cost, the identity coupling gives zero transport cost, whereas a finite information budget limits how closely the two copies can track each other. If their common mean is m and covariance is Σ, then 

E ∥X − Y ∥2 = 2 Tr Σ − 2E⟨X − m, Y − m⟩. (2) Minimizing transport cost thus amounts to maximizing self-correlation: how much correlation can a given mutual information budget support? We establish a sharp Gaussian benchmark for this question. The information-theoretic roots of (1) go back to Shannon’s source-coding theorems and the rate-distortion formalism [2]–[5]. In classical rate-distortion theory, for a source X ∼ μ, one minimizes the expected distortion Ec(X, bX) over conditional distributions P bX|X subject to a mutual information constraint I(X; bX) ≤ R; equivalently, the source marginal μ is fixed while the reproduction marginal is left free to be optimized. If the reproduction marginal is additionally prescribed to be ν, the resulting problem is precisely of the form (1). Such output-constrained source-coding problems were studied in [6]. The viewpoint of interpreting (1) as an information-constrained optimal transport problem, and its connection to transport– entropy theory, was introduced in [1]. In particular, [1] showed that this viewpoint yields a strengthening and information-constrained generalization of Talagrand’s Gaussian transportation inequality, which bounds quadratic transport cost to a Gaussian reference in terms of relative entropy [7]–[9]. In the same work, the strengthened inequality was applied to derive a relay-channel converse, recovering the resolution of Cover’s relay-channel problem in the Gaussian case [10]. A Lagrangian relaxation of the mutual-information constraint in (1) leads to entropy-regularized optimal transport and its computational developments, including Sinkhorn regularization [13], [14]. In this work, we study the information-constrained optimal transport problem in the special case where the two prescribed marginals are the same. Under quadratic cost, this self-coupling problem amounts to asking how much correlation can be created between two copies of a given distribution using a limited amount of mutual information. For a scalar law μ with mean 

m and variance σ2 > 0, our main result takes the form 

sup   

> π∈Π( μ,μ )
> Iπ(X;Y)≤R

Eπ [( X − m)( Y − m)] ≥ σ2p1 − e−2R. (3) The Gaussian law attains equality, whereas every non-Gaussian scalar law satisfies a strict inequality for R > 0. Thus, among distributions with a given variance, the Gaussian requires the most mutual information to achieve a prescribed level of self-correlation. More generally, for a multivariate law with covariance matrix Σ ≻ 0, our main theorem shows that the minimum mutual information required to achieve a prescribed positive semidefinite cross-covariance target 0 ⪯ S ≺ Σ is no larger than that required for a Gaussian law with the same covariance matrix. The comparison is strict for non-Gaussian laws whenever S ≻ 0.This result belongs to a broader family of Gaussian extremality phenomena in information theory. Under covariance constraints, Gaussian distributions maximize entropy and play extremal roles in rate-distortion theory and many information inequalities; related Gaussian comparison principles have been developed more recently in [15], [16]. The self-coupling problem considered here, however, poses a different type of extremal question: the marginal distribution itself is prescribed, and the optimization is over the dependence between two copies of that law. Even when the common marginal is Gaussian, admissible couplings need not be jointly Gaussian, so establishing a sharp Gaussian comparison requires controlling the full class of couplings. The appearance of correlation invites a comparison with Hirschfeld–Gebelein–R´ enyi maximal correlation [17]–[19]. Maximal correlation optimizes over square-integrable transforms of a fixed pair of random variables. In (3), by contrast, we optimize over joint laws with prescribed marginals. One might be inclined to prove (3) by starting with a correlated Gaussian pair that attains equality in the bound and pushing both coordinates through a nonlinear transport map to the target marginal μ. The resulting coupling, however, may lose correlation and thus fail to establish the desired bound. Indeed, the Hermite expansion and Mehler formula show that nonlinear components decay faster than linear ones [20]. We therefore construct the dependence directly from the prescribed marginal law μ.Our proof begins by relaxing the correlation constraint via convex duality. Changing to a correlated Gaussian reference then absorbs the quadratic term in the objective, reducing the comparison to an upper bound on the minimum relative entropy over self-couplings. To obtain this bound, we send each endpoint law through the same Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) smoothing kernel. The resulting half-bridges share an intermediate marginal and can therefore be joined by sampling their endpoints independently conditional on a common midpoint. This construction preserves both endpoint laws. The relative entropy of the path law is the sum of the two endpoint relative entropies minus the relative entropy of the shared midpoint. Projecting onto the endpoints then gives the coupling bound needed for the dual comparison. The midpoint relative entropy also provides the gain that makes the inequality strict for non-Gaussian laws when the cross-covariance target is positive definite. The self-coupling inequality has a direct application to rate-distortion-perception (RDP) theory [21], initiated by Blau and Michaeli through the introduction of a distributional perception constraint into the rate-distortion (RD) framework [22]. Following Freirich et al. [23], in this work we consider quadratic distortion and squared Wasserstein-2 perception constraints. Under perfect perception, the reconstruction must have the source law, so the self-coupling inequality provides a starting point. Allowing the two laws to differ permits a reduction in distortion by shrinking the reconstruction toward the source mean. This displacement controls the perception loss, while the self-correlation bound controls distortion; the operation requires no additional mutual information. Choosing the amount of shrinkage appropriately gives a simple proof that, among sources with the same mean and covariance, the Gaussian has the largest minimum achievable distortion. The comparison is strict for non-Gaussian sources whenever the Gaussian optimal rate allocation assigns positive rate to every covariance eigendirection. A further question arises when the randomness shared by the encoder and decoder is limited [24]–[27]. The single-letter characterization of the relevant RDP limit in [28] involves two couplings linking the respective endpoints to a common auxiliary random variable, with a separate mutual information constraint on each coupling. We use a variational identity to recast this characterization in the form of a weighted sum of quadratic errors, bringing the comparison back to moments of a self-coupling. To accommodate the unequal information budgets, we retain an intermediate observation on one half-bridge. Simultaneous rate balancing, justified by the Poincar´ e–Miranda theorem, selects observation strengths that satisfy both information constraints and the required moment bounds [29], [30]. This proves Gaussian extremality for every finite common-randomness budget, with strictness for non-Gaussian sources under the same positive rate allocation condition on the Gaussian optimizer. The paper is organized as follows. Section II proves the self-coupling inequality and discusses its consequences. Section III applies the inequality to establish Gaussian extremality for quadratic Wasserstein RDP with unlimited common randomness. Section IV treats the more challenging setting of limited common randomness. Section V concludes the paper. The appendices provide supporting arguments on duality, approximation, Gaussian optimization, continuity, and variational identities. 3

V. C ONCLUSION 

The self-coupling inequality gives a sharp Gaussian comparison for the information cost of imposing self-correlation. Its proof constructs two half-bridges with the same midpoint law and joins their conditional endpoint distributions. The relative entropy of the shared midpoint yields a gain that, through a Gaussian change of reference and convex duality, leads to a matrix cross-covariance bound. For non-Gaussian laws, the information comparison is strict whenever the target cross-covariance is positive definite. In particular, every non-Gaussian scalar law admits strictly greater self-correlation than the Gaussian benchmark at every positive information budget. The quadratic RDP comparison follows by scaling one endpoint of the self-coupling. With finite common randomness, two information budgets constrain the endpoints’ dependence on a shared auxiliary random variable. A variational formula for the squared Wasserstein-2 perception penalty reduces the problem to comparing two weighted quadratic errors. A marked midpoint bridge, with its rates balanced simultaneously using the Poincar´ e–Miranda theorem, then establishes Gaussian extremality in this setting as well. Both distortion comparisons are strict for non-Gaussian sources when a Gaussian minimizing allocation assigns positive rate to every covariance eigendirection. The shared midpoint entropy also raises questions about quantitative stability of these comparisons. More broadly, gluing several conditional endpoint laws over a common intermediate distribution may help extend the analysis to self-coupling problems with more than two copies. In both directions, the key idea is to coordinate the endpoint constraints through a shared intermediate law.
